## Overview

The horn of plenty: a goat's or river god's horn overflowing with fruit, grain and flowers, the classical symbol of abundance and good fortune. Carried by goddesses of luck and harvest, it lives on in harvest festivals, Thanksgiving tables and the coats of arms of several nations. [src: wp]

## Myth & Origin

The Greeks told two stories. In one, the infant Zeus was hidden from his father Cronus on Crete and nursed by the goat (or the nymph) Amalthea. Playing, he broke off one of her horns, and gave it the power to fill itself with whatever its owner wished. [src: wp]

In the other, told by Ovid, Heracles wrestled the river god Achelous for the hand of Deianira. Achelous turned into a serpent, then a bull; Heracles tore off one of his horns, and the Naiads filled it with fruit and flowers and made it sacred to Plenty. [src: ovid, wp]

The Romans called it *cornu copiae*, "horn of plenty", and gave it to Abundantia, to Fortuna, to Ceres, and to Plutus, god of wealth. [src: wp]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

Greek and Roman art put the horn in the arms of gods of the earth, harvest and fortune, of rivers, and of personified cities and provinces. Roman coins used it to promise prosperity under an emperor; two crossed cornucopias meant a doubly blessed age. [src: wp]

### Medieval

The horn survived in learned allegory and in manuscripts as an attribute of Abundance, Fortune and Charity. [src: wp]

### Early modern

Renaissance and Baroque art loved it: in allegorical paintings, on fountains, in emblem books, and in the hands of figures of Peace and Plenty. [src: wp]

### Modern

The cornucopia became a centrepiece of harvest festivals and of American Thanksgiving, usually as a woven basket horn spilling autumn produce. Newly independent Latin American nations put it in their coats of arms: Colombia, Peru (where it spills gold coins) and Venezuela among them. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Thanksgiving and harvest:** table centrepieces, decorations and greeting cards.
- **National emblems:** the coats of arms of Colombia, Peru and Venezuela, and many state and city seals.
- **Language:** "a cornucopia of" means a rich abundance of anything.
- **Design:** logos for food, banking and agriculture.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Greek and Roman

Divine abundance and the bounty of the earth, owed to the gods; with Fortuna, luck that can be given and taken away. [src: wp]

### Renaissance allegory

Peace, prosperity and good government: the horn in the hands of Peace means war has ended and fields can be farmed. [src: wp]

### Modern

Gratitude for the harvest, and a national promise of natural wealth. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Which horn?

- **Amalthea's.** The older and more widespread story ties it to Zeus's nurse. [src: wp]
- **Achelous's.** Ovid and others give it to the river god defeated by Heracles. Ancient writers already mixed the two. [src: ovid]

### A sign of real or false wealth?

- **Abundance as blessing.** In ancient and harvest symbolism the horn means gratitude and shared plenty. [src: wp]
- **A warning.** Moralists, from ancient satirists to modern critics of consumption, used the overflowing horn to question greed and waste. [src: wp]

## Article

The cornucopia is abundance you can hold in one arm. A horn, the hard, curved weapon of a goat or bull, turned upside down and filled with apples, grapes, pomegranates and ears of wheat. The image is so satisfying that it has barely changed in two and a half thousand years: a Roman coin and a Thanksgiving table show the same thing.

Its two myths explain plenty in different ways. In one, plenty is a nurse's gift. The baby god breaks his foster-mother's horn by accident, and turns the damage into blessing: whoever holds it will never lack. In the other, plenty is won from a river by a hero. Rivers make fields fertile; Heracles wrestles the river and takes its horn; the nymphs fill it with fruit. Both stories link wealth to nature, to nourishing and to struggle.

In Roman hands the horn became political. Emperors put it on coins to promise good harvests and peace; two crossed horns meant a golden age. The same promise appears in the coats of arms of Colombia, Peru and Venezuela, made by young republics that wanted to say: this land is rich.

The cornucopia is generous to a fault, and that is its risk. A horn that always spills invites the question every abundance story asks: who gets to eat, and what happens when the horn runs dry?

For a storyteller it is a simple, warm object with a hero's story inside it: a horn torn from a god.
