## Overview

The legendary sword of Japan's imperial regalia, with the mirror Yata no Kagami and the jewel Yasakani no Magatama. The storm god Susanoo found it inside the tail of an eight-headed serpent; the hero Yamato Takeru used it to cut the grass around him when enemies set a field on fire, and it took his name. It is kept hidden at Atsuta Shrine in Nagoya and has not been seen in public for centuries. [src: wp, kojiki]

## Myth & Origin

Banished from heaven for his violence against his sister, the sun goddess Amaterasu, the storm god Susanoo came down to Izumo. There he met an old couple weeping: the eight-headed, eight-tailed serpent Yamata no Orochi had eaten seven of their eight daughters, and was coming for the last. Susanoo turned the girl into a comb in his hair, set out eight vats of strong sake, and when each head drank and fell asleep, he cut the serpent to pieces. In one tail his sword struck something hard: a great sword, which he named Ame-no-Murakumo, "Gathering Clouds of Heaven", and offered to Amaterasu as a peace gift. [src: kojiki, wp]

Generations later the sword was given to the prince Yamato Takeru as he set out to subdue the eastern lands. In Sagami (or Suruga), enemies lured him into a grassy plain and set it on fire. He cut the grass around him with the sword (in some tellings the sword moved by itself) and lit a counter-fire with flints his aunt had given him. From then on it was called Kusanagi, the "Grass-Cutter". He left it with his wife Miyazu-hime before his last expedition, and died without it; it was enshrined at Atsuta. [src: kojiki, wp]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

Bronze and iron swords were prestige objects in ancient Japan, deposited in tombs and shrines; the regalia of mirror, jewel and sword reflect this older world. The *Kojiki* (712) and *Nihon Shoki* (720) set down the myths at the court of Nara. [src: wp]

### Medieval

The *Nihon Shoki* records that in 668 a monk stole the sword from Atsuta, but a storm forced him back. At the sea battle of Dan-no-ura in 1185, the child emperor Antoku was drowned with the regalia; the sword aboard was lost. Court tradition held that it was a replica, the original remaining at Atsuta, and a new replica was made. [src: wp]

### Early modern

The sword stayed hidden at Atsuta, seen only by priests; a few stories, not officially confirmed, tell of priests who looked and fell ill or died. [src: wp]

### Modern

The regalia remain central to imperial accession. At Emperor Naruhito's accession in 2019 the sword and jewel, wrapped and boxed, were presented in the ceremony; no one sees the sword itself. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Shinto and the imperial house:** for many Japanese it is a sacred treasure, a sign of the continuity of the imperial line and of the gods' protection. Atsuta Shrine, where it is kept, is visited by millions.
- **Popular culture:** anime, manga and games are full of swords named Kusanagi.
- **Names:** used for characters, ships and products.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Shinto and court ideology

The three regalia, given by Amaterasu to her grandson when he descended to rule the earth, prove the emperor's divine descent; the sword stands for valour or power. [src: wp]

### As a myth of iron and smiths

Scholars have read the serpent with a sword in its tail as a memory of iron-working in Izumo (whose rivers ran red with iron sand), or of taming floods. [src: wp]

### As a story of the hero's protector

In Yamato Takeru's tale the sword saves him from fire, and his death comes after he leaves it behind: an object of divine protection that should not be set aside. [src: kojiki]

## Debates

### Does the sword still exist?

- **Yes, at Atsuta.** The shrine and the imperial household hold that the original is kept there. [src: wp]
- **Unknowable.** It has not been shown or examined; descriptions come from old accounts. [src: wp]

### Was the sword lost at Dan-no-ura the original?

- **A replica.** Court tradition says the palace copy was lost and the original stayed at Atsuta. [src: wp]
- **The real one.** Some medieval accounts treat the loss as real, which later tradition explained away. [src: wp]

### What does the serpent story remember?

- **Iron-working in Izumo.** The sword inside the serpent as iron won from the rivers. [src: wp]
- **Flood control.** The eight-headed serpent as a river with many branches, tamed by a hero. [src: wp]
- **Myth, not history.** Others warn against reading the myth too literally. [src: wp]

## Article

Kusanagi is a sword that hides. It is found hidden, in the tail of a monster; it spends most of its history hidden, wrapped and boxed in a shrine; and no living person outside a few priests has seen it. Its power seems to depend on that invisibility. A national treasure that is never shown is also never doubted.

Its two stories show two faces of the sacred sword. Susanoo is a wild, destructive god who redeems himself by killing the serpent and giving the sword to his sister: a gift of reconciliation between storm and sun. Yamato Takeru is a tragic hero whose life is saved by the sword in the burning grass, and lost when he goes to his last fight without it. The sword protects, but only those who keep it close.

As one of the three imperial regalia, with a mirror and a jewel, it belongs to a set: the mirror that drew the sun goddess out of her cave, the curved jewel, and the sword from the serpent. Together they say that the emperor holds the light of heaven, its beauty and its strength.

The sinking at Dan-no-ura, where the child emperor drowned holding the regalia, gave the sword a tragic history that mixes with the myth. Was it the true sword? Court tradition said no, the true one was safe; and so the hidden sword survived even its own loss.

For a storyteller, Kusanagi shows the power of an object that is always offstage: the more completely it is hidden, the more people believe in it.
