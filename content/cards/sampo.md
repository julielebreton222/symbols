## Overview

A magic object of Finnish epic poetry, forged by the smith Ilmarinen, that grinds out grain, salt and money without end and brings prosperity to whoever holds it. Stolen back from the dark north, it breaks in a sea battle; its fragments wash ashore and make Finland fertile. Nobody, ancient or modern, quite agrees what it was. [src: kalevala, wp]

## Myth & Origin

In the *Kalevala*, Louhi, the fearsome mistress of Pohjola (the North), promises her daughter in marriage to whoever can forge a Sampo. The old sage Väinämöinen sends the smith Ilmarinen, who forges it from the tip of a swan's feather, the milk of a barren cow, a grain of barley and the wool of a summer ewe. It has a "many-coloured lid" (*kirjokansi*), and it grinds a measure of grain for eating, one for selling and one for keeping. Louhi locks it in a hill of copper behind nine locks, and it puts down roots nine fathoms deep. [src: kalevala]

Ilmarinen does not win the girl at first, and Pohjola grows rich. Later Väinämöinen, Ilmarinen and the reckless Lemminkäinen sail north to steal it back. Väinämöinen sings Pohjola to sleep and they carry it off, but Louhi pursues them, turning herself into a giant eagle; in the struggle the Sampo falls and shatters. Most pieces sink into the sea (which is why the sea is rich), and a few wash up on the Finnish shore, bringing luck to the land. [src: kalevala]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

The Sampo belongs to Finnic oral poetry, sung for centuries in Karelia and Ingria in the old trochaic metre; how far back the songs go is unknown. [src: wp]

### Medieval

No medieval written sources mention it; the singing tradition continued among peasants on both sides of the Finnish-Russian border. [src: wp]

### Early modern

Collectors began recording the runes in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. [src: wp]

### Modern

Elias Lönnrot wove the songs he collected into the *Kalevala* (1835, enlarged 1849), which became Finland's national epic and a pillar of Finnish identity during the struggle for independence (1917). Akseli Gallen-Kallela painted *The Defence of the Sampo* (1896). The Sampo has since given its name to banks, ships and a Soviet-Finnish fantasy film (1959). [src: wp]

## Today

- **National culture:** an image of Finnish prosperity, ingenuity and the Kalevala heritage; Kalevala Day is celebrated every 28 February.
- **Names:** banks, insurers, icebreakers, companies and products called Sampo.
- **Art and music:** paintings, operas, rock and metal songs.
- **Scholarship:** a classic puzzle for folklorists and mythologists.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### In the songs

A source of plenty and luck that a people must win and keep. Its loss and scattering explain why the sea and the land are fertile. [src: kalevala]

### Comparative mythology

Scholars have compared it with the Norse mill Grotti, which ground out gold and peace for King Fróði until it was taken to sea and ground salt until it sank. [src: wp]

### As a cosmic image

Others read the Sampo as the world pillar holding up the sky, with its "many-coloured lid" as the starry dome. [src: wp]

## Debates

### What was the Sampo?

Dozens of theories have been proposed.

- **A mill.** It grinds grain, salt and money; compare Grotti. [src: wp]
- **The world pillar or sky.** The many-coloured lid as the vault of heaven turning around the pole; held by scholars such as Uno Harva. [src: wp]
- **A treasure or talisman.** Some have proposed a decorated shield, an idol or a coin-making device. [src: wp]
- **Deliberately mysterious.** It may never have been a single object: the singers themselves may not have pictured it. [src: wp]

### Folk epic or national invention?

- **A folk epic.** Built from genuine traditional songs. [src: wp]
- **Lönnrot's creation.** The *Kalevala* is a nineteenth-century compilation and rewriting that joined separate songs into a story they never told together. [src: wp]

## Article

The Sampo is a machine of plenty whose shape nobody knows. The poems tell us what went into it (a swan's feather, a barren cow's milk, one barley grain, a summer ewe's wool) and what comes out of it: food to eat, food to sell, food to keep. They give it a lid of many colours and roots that grow into the hill. But whether it is a mill, a pillar, a shield or something else, the singers do not say. Perhaps they never needed to.

What matters is its effect. Whoever holds the Sampo is rich. The story is really about who gets to be rich: the dark North that hoards it, or the heroes of Kalevala who take it back. It is a story about luck and its unfair distribution, and like many such stories it ends in a struggle that breaks the thing everyone wanted.

The ending is the most beautiful part. The Sampo shatters; most of it sinks, and that is why the sea is full of fish; a few fragments wash up, and that is why Finland's fields yield bread. Wealth that was locked in one hill is scattered through the world. The destruction of the treasure becomes the blessing of the land.

When Lönnrot published the *Kalevala* in the nineteenth century, the Sampo became more than a myth: an image of what Finland could be, a small nation with hidden riches, taking back what was theirs.

For a storyteller, the Sampo is a model of the treasure whose mystery is part of its power, and of the quest that succeeds by failing: no one keeps it, so everyone shares it.
