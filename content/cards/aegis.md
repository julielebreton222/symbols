## Overview

The terrifying shield or goatskin cloak of Zeus, worn also by his daughter Athena, fringed with golden tassels or snakes and bearing the Gorgon's head. When it is shaken, armies panic. In English, to act "under the aegis" of someone means under their protection. [src: wp, iliad]

## Myth & Origin

In Homer's *Iliad* the aegis is Zeus's, made for him by the smith-god Hephaestus. Zeus lends it to Apollo and to Athena; when it is shaken thunder rolls and the Achaeans flee. When Athena arms for battle she throws it around her shoulders: tasselled, terrible, with Rout, Strife, Force and Pursuit wreathed around it, and in its midst the head of the Gorgon. [src: iliad, wp]

Later myth explains the Gorgon's head. The hero Perseus, helped by Athena and Hermes, cut off the head of Medusa, whose gaze turned people to stone, and gave it to Athena, who set it on her aegis. Some ancient writers said the aegis was the skin of the goat Amalthea, who nursed the infant Zeus; others, of a monster Athena killed. [src: bulfinch, wp]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

The word is related to Greek *aix*, "goat". In art from the sixth century BCE, Athena wears the aegis as a short scaly cape edged with snakes, the Gorgon's face on her breast, as on the Parthenon's great statue of Athena Parthenos. The historian Herodotus (5th century BCE) thought the Greeks had copied it from the fringed goatskin garments of Libyan women. Roman emperors were shown wearing the aegis as a sign of divine protection. [src: wp]

### Medieval

Classical myth survived in learned books and allegories; the Gorgon-head shield passed into the general image of Minerva as Wisdom. [src: wp]

### Early modern

Renaissance art showed Minerva and Athena with the aegis and Medusa's head; the phrase "under the aegis of" entered English in the eighteenth century. [src: wp]

### Modern

The word became a general term for protection and sponsorship, and a favourite name for defence systems, insurers, security companies and software, including the US Navy's Aegis Combat System. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Language:** "under the aegis of the United Nations", "under the aegis of the museum".
- **Defence and security:** the Aegis Combat System; many companies and products.
- **Art and heraldry:** the gorgoneion on shields, coins and emblems.
- **Games and fiction:** a common item in fantasy and video games, often as an all-protecting shield.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Homeric: the storm and terror of Zeus

The aegis is weather and fear: shaken over the battlefield, it brings the storm and breaks an army's nerve. [src: iliad]

### Athena's protection

On Athena it becomes the sign of the goddess who protects cities and heroes, wise and armed. The Gorgon's head is an apotropaic image: a terror turned outward to keep evil away. [src: wp]

### Psychoanalytic

Freud's short essay "Medusa's Head" (written 1922) read the Gorgon's head as a symbol of castration terror, and its use on Athena's aegis as a sign that she is untouchable. One modern reading among many. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Shield or cloak?

- **A shield.** Many translators and Roman writers picture a shield. [src: wp]
- **A goatskin cloak or cape.** Greek art usually shows a cape worn over Athena's shoulders, and the word suggests goatskin. [src: wp]

### Libyan origin?

- **Yes.** Herodotus said the Greeks took the aegis from the goatskin dress of Libyan women. [src: wp]
- **Not proven.** Modern scholars treat this as one ancient guess about an image that may have many sources. [src: wp]

## Article

The aegis is protection that frightens. Most shields keep blows off; this one wins battles by being seen. When Zeus or Apollo shakes it, the sky thunders and the enemy's courage drains away. When Athena wears it, a ring of personified terrors (Rout, Strife, Force, Pursuit) runs around its edge, and in the middle stares the Gorgon. It is armour made of fear.

The Gorgon's head shows how the Greeks thought about danger. Medusa's face turns those who look at it to stone; Perseus could kill her only by looking at her reflection. Once cut off, the deadly face becomes a protective one, worn by the goddess of wisdom and painted on countless shields and doorways. What threatens you can, once mastered, guard you. The same idea lives in the evil eye and the hamsa.

The aegis is also a lesson in how words outlive things. Almost nobody today pictures a goatskin cloak fringed with snakes, but newspapers speak every day of conferences held "under the aegis" of governments. The terror has gone; the protection remains.

For a storyteller, the aegis is a gift for scenes of power: an object that need not touch anyone to defeat them, and a face that is both the monster and the shield against monsters.
