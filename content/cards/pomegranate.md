## Overview

A red fruit full of hundreds of jewel-like seeds: in Greek myth the fruit that bound Persephone to the underworld; in the Bible, the ornament of the high priest's robe and Solomon's Temple; in Christian art, a sign of the Resurrection and the Church; in Persian, Armenian and Islamic culture, of plenty and paradise. Almost everywhere it stands for fertility, abundance and the meeting of life and death. [src: wp, hymn]

## Myth & Origin

In the *Homeric Hymn to Demeter*, Hades carries off Persephone to the underworld. Her mother Demeter, goddess of grain, grieves and lets the earth go barren until Zeus orders Hades to return the girl. But Hades secretly gives Persephone a pomegranate seed to eat, and because she has eaten the food of the dead, she must return to him for part of every year. While she is below, the earth is in winter. [src: hymn]

In the Hebrew Bible, golden bells and woven pomegranates alternate on the hem of the high priest's robe (Exodus 28), and rows of bronze pomegranates crowned the pillars of Solomon's Temple (1 Kings 7). The spies sent into Canaan brought back pomegranates as a sign of the land's richness. [src: bible]

The Qur'an names pomegranates among the fruits of the gardens of Paradise. [src: quran]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

Native to Iran and the region around it, the pomegranate was cultivated very early; dried fruits were placed in Egyptian tombs. In Greece it belonged to Persephone and to Hera, whose statue at Argos held one. Pomegranates decorate Phoenician, Hebrew and Persian art and coins. [src: wp]

### Medieval

Christian art made the burst pomegranate a sign of the Resurrection and of the Church (many seeds in one fruit). In the Islamic world it became a motif of textiles, tiles and poetry, and gave its name to Granada, whose emblem it is. [src: wp]

### Early modern

Renaissance painters placed it in the hands of the infant Christ and the Virgin, as in Botticelli's *Madonna of the Pomegranate* (c. 1487). It appears in the coat of arms of Catherine of Aragon and of the city and kingdom of Granada. [src: wp]

### Modern

In Armenia and Iran it is a national symbol; Sergei Parajanov's film *The Color of Pomegranates* (1969) made it the emblem of Armenian art. It became a "superfood" of the twenty-first century. In Jewish tradition it is eaten at Rosh Hashanah with the wish that one's merits be as many as its seeds. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Jewish practice:** eaten at the New Year; silver finials on the Torah scroll are called *rimonim*, "pomegranates". A popular saying claims it has 613 seeds, like the commandments of the Torah.
- **Christian art:** a sign of the Resurrection and of the Church in churches and icons.
- **Iran and Armenia:** central to festivals (such as the Persian winter-solstice night, Yalda), weddings and art.
- **Granada:** the city's name and emblem.
- **Food and health:** juice, molasses and seeds in cuisines from Iran to the Mediterranean.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Greek myth

The food that binds: to eat the fruit of a place is to belong to it. The pomegranate's red juice links it with blood, marriage and death. [src: hymn]

### Hebrew and Jewish

Abundance, beauty, the fruitfulness of the promised land, and later the commandments: many seeds, many good deeds. [src: bible]

### Christian

The Resurrection (the fruit bursts open with life), and the unity of the many in one Church. [src: wp]

### Persian and Islamic

Fruit of paradise, fertility and celebration. [src: quran]

## Debates

### Was the forbidden fruit a pomegranate?

- **An apple.** Western art made it an apple, partly through a Latin pun (*malum*, "evil" and "apple"). [src: wp]
- **A pomegranate, a fig or a grape.** Jewish and other interpreters have proposed these; Genesis never names the fruit. [src: wp]

### Does it really have 613 seeds?

- **A beautiful saying.** The number of seeds varies widely from fruit to fruit; the 613 is a symbol, not a botanical fact. [src: wp]

### How many seeds did Persephone eat?

- **One, or a few.** The Homeric Hymn speaks of a single seed; later writers give three, four, six or seven, which then set the months she spends below. [src: hymn, wp]

## Article

Cut a pomegranate open and you see the reason for its long symbolic career: a leathery shell full of hundreds of red, glistening seeds packed in white pith. It looks like treasure, like blood, like a crowd. Few fruits are so obviously full of life, and so obviously wounded when they are opened.

For the Greeks it was the fruit of Persephone. One seed, eaten in the underworld, ties the young goddess to the dead for part of every year, and so gives the world its seasons. It is a story about consent and trickery, marriage and abduction, mother and daughter, and the pomegranate stands at its centre as the price of belonging.

In the Bible the same fruit means blessing. It hangs embroidered from the high priest's hem, between golden bells; it crowns the pillars of Solomon's Temple; the spies carry it back from the promised land. Later Jewish tradition counted its seeds as the commandments, a fruit full of good deeds.

Christians, Persians, Armenians and Muslims each made it their own: the Resurrection bursting open, a fruit of paradise, the red heart of a nation. That is perhaps its gift: a symbol of abundance that has never belonged to only one people.

For a writer, the pomegranate is a perfect object for stories of thresholds: one seed is enough to change where you belong.
