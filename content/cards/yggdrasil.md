## Overview

The immense ash tree at the centre of the Norse cosmos, whose branches spread over all the worlds and whose roots reach down to three wells. Gods hold court beneath it, Norns water it, beasts gnaw it, and Odin hung on it to win the runes. At the end of the world it shudders, but in some tellings it shelters the two humans who survive. [src: wp, poetic, prose]

## Myth & Origin

The *Völuspá*, the seeress's prophecy in the *Poetic Edda*, sees "an ash called Yggdrasil", ever green, standing over the well of Urd, where the three Norns shape the lives of men. Snorri Sturluson's *Prose Edda* (c. 1220) gives it three roots: one among the gods over the well of Urd, one among the frost giants over Mímir's well of wisdom, and one in Niflheim over the spring Hvergelmir, where the dragon Níðhöggr gnaws it. [src: poetic, prose]

The tree is alive with creatures. An eagle sits in its top with a hawk between its eyes; the squirrel Ratatoskr runs up and down carrying insults between the eagle and the dragon; four stags browse its leaves; snakes gnaw its roots. The Norns pour water and white clay from the well over it every day so that it does not rot. The poem *Grímnismál* says the tree suffers more than anyone knows. [src: poetic, prose]

In the *Hávamál* Odin tells how he hung on a windswept tree for nine nights, wounded by a spear, given to himself, and then reached down and took up the runes. The name *Yggdrasil* is often read as "Ygg's horse", Ygg being one of Odin's names and "horse" an old poetic word for the gallows. [src: poetic, wp]

At Ragnarök, the *Völuspá* says, the ancient tree trembles and groans. Snorri adds that two humans, Líf and Lífþrasir, will hide in "Hoddmímir's wood" and survive to repeople the earth. [src: poetic, prose]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

Sacred trees and groves were part of Germanic religion from Roman times; the historian Tacitus (1st century CE) describes rites in holy groves. [src: wp]

### Medieval

The Saxon sacred pillar Irminsul, perhaps also a world-pillar, was destroyed by Charlemagne in 772. Adam of Bremen (c. 1070) describes a great evergreen tree and a well beside the pagan temple at Uppsala. The Eddic poems that name Yggdrasil were written down in thirteenth-century Iceland, two centuries after the conversion. [src: wp]

### Early modern

Scandinavian scholars published the Eddas, and the World Ash entered European learning; a seventeenth-century Icelandic manuscript drawing of the tree is one of the first images of it. [src: wp]

### Modern

The Romantic and nationalist revivals painted and sang of the World Ash; Wagner's *Ring* opens with the Norns weaving at the ash's foot. In the twentieth century Yggdrasil became a central symbol of Heathenry and a favourite image in fantasy, games and tattoos. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Faith:** for many Heathens, the image of the cosmos and of the interconnection of all beings, used in ritual and art.
- **Fiction and games:** fantasy novels, comics, anime and video games (including *God of War*) build worlds around it.
- **Design and tattoos:** among the most common Norse motifs, often with the runic and knot work of Viking art.
- **Science and technology:** the name of software, data systems and research projects about networks and trees of knowledge.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### In the Eddas

A living, suffering axis that holds the worlds together: watered by fate (the Norns), gnawed by destruction, and connecting gods, giants, humans and the dead. [src: poetic, prose]

### Comparative religion

Mircea Eliade made the world tree a classic example of the *axis mundi*, the centre where heaven, earth and underworld meet, and compared it with shamanic trees climbed by Siberian shamans. See the reference books' readings of the World Tree and World Axis. [src: wp]

### Christian influence?

Some scholars have read Odin hanging on the tree and wounded by a spear as shaped by the Crucifixion; others defend it as a pagan myth that Christians later found strikingly familiar. [src: wp]

## Debates

### What does the name mean?

- **"Odin's horse".** *Ygg* (the Terrible One, a name of Odin) and *drasill* (horse), meaning the gallows Odin "rode" when he hung on the tree. The most common view. [src: wp]
- **"Yew pillar".** From an older word for yew, with the tree as a cosmic column. [src: wp]
- **"Terror tree".** From *yggr*, terror, without reference to Odin. [src: wp]

### Ash or yew?

- **An ash.** The sources call it *askr*, ash. [src: poetic]
- **Originally a yew.** Some scholars note that it is "ever green", which fits the evergreen yew better than the ash. [src: wp]

### Pagan or Christian?

- **A pre-Christian world tree,** paralleled by the Irminsul and the Uppsala tree. [src: wp]
- **Coloured by Christianity.** The poems were written down by Christians, and some details may echo the Cross. [src: wp]

## Article

Yggdrasil is a tree that hurts. That is the detail that makes it unforgettable. The gods meet in council under its branches, the Norns weave fate at its feet, and yet "the ash Yggdrasil suffers more than men know": a dragon gnaws its roots, stags strip its leaves, its trunk rots at the side. The world is held together by something wounded, and kept alive only by daily care. Every morning the Norns pour water and white clay over it.

The tree is also a network. A squirrel runs its whole height, from the eagle at the top to the dragon at the bottom, carrying insults. It is a comic detail, and a profound one: the parts of the world are connected even by their quarrels. Information, enmity and fate all travel up and down the same trunk.

Odin's ordeal makes the tree a place of knowledge. He hangs on it for nine nights, wounded, "given to myself", until he sees the runes and takes them up. Wisdom here is not studied but suffered; the god pays with his own life to gain it. Whether or not the poem knew the story of the Crucifixion, it understood that the axis of the world is also a place of sacrifice.

And at the end, when the wolf is loose and the gods are dying, the old tree trembles but does not quite fall. In Snorri's telling two humans hide in the wood and come out into a green, new world.

For a storyteller, Yggdrasil is a model of a setting that is also a character: a world with a centre that can be climbed, wounded, watered and, at the end, survived.
