## Overview

A plain staff with a single snake coiled around it, carried by Asclepius, the Greek god of healing. It is the true traditional emblem of medicine, used by the World Health Organization, many national medical associations and the "Star of Life" on ambulances. [src: wp]

## Myth & Origin

Asclepius was the son of Apollo and the mortal Coronis. Apollo, told that Coronis was unfaithful, had her killed, and snatched the unborn child from her body on the funeral pyre. The boy was raised by the wise centaur Chiron, who taught him medicine. He became so skilled that he raised the dead, and Zeus, fearing that mortals would escape death, struck him with a thunderbolt. Afterwards he was honoured as a god. [src: wp, bulfinch]

His sanctuaries, the *Asklepieia*, were places of healing; the most famous was at Epidaurus. Sick pilgrims slept in the temple and hoped the god would visit them in a dream, often in the form of a snake. Harmless snakes (today called the Aesculapian snake) lived in the sanctuaries. [src: wp]

In 293 BCE, during a plague, the Romans brought the god's cult from Epidaurus. Ovid tells how the god travelled as a great serpent on the ship and, as it sailed up the Tiber, slipped off onto the island in the river, where his temple was built. [src: ovid, wp]

The Hippocratic Oath, the ancient oath of physicians, begins by swearing "by Apollo the physician, and Asclepius, and Hygieia and Panacea". [src: wp]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

Statues and reliefs from the fifth and fourth centuries BCE show Asclepius as a calm, bearded man leaning on a staff with a snake. His daughters Hygieia (health) and Panacea (cure-all) were worshipped with him. The cult spread across the Greek and Roman world. [src: wp]

### Medieval

As Christianity replaced the old cults, the god faded, but his staff survived in learned books on medicine and in the memory of the Hippocratic tradition. [src: wp]

### Early modern

Renaissance physicians, reviving ancient learning, took up Asclepius and his staff as emblems of their art in books and on institutions. [src: wp]

### Modern

Twentieth-century medicine made the rod its international sign: the World Health Organization's emblem (1948) puts it on the UN globe and laurels; the "Star of Life" of emergency medical services bears it; many medical associations, including the American Medical Association, use it. The [[caduceus]] competes with it, especially in the United States. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Medicine worldwide:** the emblem of the World Health Organization, of many national medical associations and of medical faculties.
- **Ambulances:** the blue six-armed "Star of Life" carries the rod.
- **Pharmacy and veterinary medicine:** variations of the rod, sometimes with a cup (the bowl of Hygieia, the sign of pharmacy).
- **Tattoos and jewellery:** popular with doctors, nurses and paramedics.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Ancient: the healing god's companion

The snake was sacred to Asclepius; it lived in his temples and visited the dreams of the sick. Ancient writers already explained it as a sign of renewal, because snakes shed their skins, and of watchfulness. [src: wp]

### Renaissance and after: the emblem of the profession

For physicians who saw themselves as heirs of Hippocrates, the rod meant learned, rational medicine, distinct from magic, and later, from commerce. [src: wp]

### Modern critics of the caduceus

For defenders of the rod, its simplicity matters: one snake, one staff, no wings; healing as patient work. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Rod or caduceus?

- **The rod is the emblem of medicine.** Held by historians of medicine and by bodies such as the WHO and the AMA. [src: wp]
- **Both are now understood.** The caduceus is widely used for medicine in practice, especially in the US. [src: wp]

### Is the snake really a worm?

- **The guinea worm theory.** Some have suggested the symbol began as a picture of the treatment for the parasitic guinea worm, which was drawn slowly out of the skin by winding it around a stick. [src: wp]
- **The snake is a snake.** Most historians note that ancient sources and images clearly show snakes, sacred to the god, and that the worm theory has no ancient evidence. [src: wp]

### A borrowing from the Bible?

- **Nehushtan.** Some compare the rod with the bronze serpent Moses raised on a pole to heal the Israelites (Numbers 21:8–9). [src: bible]
- **Separate traditions.** Most scholars treat the two as parallel images of the serpent's double power to wound and to heal, not as one borrowed from the other. [src: wp]

## Article

The rod of Asclepius is one of the oldest professional symbols still in use, and one of the plainest: a rough walking stick and a snake. Nothing about it is ornamental. It is the staff of a travelling doctor, the kind of man who walks from village to village with his skills.

The snake is the stranger part. Snakes kill; they also, every year, slip out of their old skin and seem to be born again. The Greeks kept harmless snakes in the healing sanctuaries, and sick people lying in the temple at night hoped the god would come to them as a serpent in a dream. The animal that brings death became the companion of the god who brings people back.

That is exactly what got Asclepius killed. In the myth he grows too good at his work and raises the dead, and Zeus, keeper of the order of things, strikes him down. It is a strange founding story for a profession: the first great healer is punished for healing too well. The myth knows something doctors still know: medicine always pushes at a limit, and the limit pushes back.

The rod has outlived the god's temples by fifteen centuries. Its quarrel with the caduceus, Hermes's twin-snaked, winged wand, is a small comedy of symbols. One staff stands for the patient work of healing; the other for messages, commerce and the guiding of souls to the dead. Doctors who care about such things insist on the single snake. The rest of the world mostly sees a staff, a serpent and a promise of help.

For a storyteller the rod holds a useful paradox: a healer marked by the creature people fear, a cure that comes from poison, a gift that the gods resent.
