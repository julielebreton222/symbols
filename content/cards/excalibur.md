## Overview

King Arthur's sword: given to him by the Lady of the Lake in some tellings, drawn from a stone in others, and thrown back into the water when he lies dying. A Welsh weapon called *Caledfwlch* became Geoffrey of Monmouth's *Caliburnus* and the French *Escalibor*, and through them the most famous sword in Western legend. [src: wp, geoffrey]

## Myth & Origin

In the Welsh tale *Culhwch and Olwen*, Arthur lists the few things he will not give away: his ship, his mantle, his sword *Caledfwlch*, his spear *Rhongomyniad*, his shield, his dagger *Carnwennan* and his wife Gwenhwyfar. The name is usually read as "hard notch" or "hard breach". [src: culhwch, wp]

Geoffrey of Monmouth's Latin *History of the Kings of Britain* (c. 1136) calls it *Caliburnus*, "the best of swords, forged in the Isle of Avalon", and gives Arthur victory with it over the Saxons. French romance turned the name into *Escalibor*. [src: geoffrey, wp]

The two great scenes come later. In Robert de Boron's *Merlin* (c. 1200) the boy Arthur proves his right to the throne by drawing a sword from a stone that no grown knight can move. In the *Post-Vulgate* romances and in Malory, that sword breaks in a fight, and Merlin takes Arthur to a lake where an arm clothed in white samite holds a sword above the water: Excalibur, the gift of the Lady of the Lake. Merlin tells him the scabbard is worth ten of the sword, for while he wears it he will lose no blood. Morgan le Fay later steals the scabbard. [src: malory1, wp]

At the end, wounded at Camlann, Arthur orders a knight (Girflet in the French *Death of Arthur*, Bedivere in Malory) to throw the sword into the lake. Twice the knight hides it, unable to waste so fine a thing; the third time he throws it, and a hand rises, catches it, brandishes it three times and draws it under. [src: malory2, wp]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

No Excalibur survives from antiquity, but people in Bronze Age and Iron Age Britain did give swords to water: many fine weapons have been found deliberately placed in rivers, lakes and bogs, such as the Iron Age hoard of Llyn Cerrig Bach on Anglesey. Some scholars see a memory of such offerings in the sword returned to the lake. [src: wp, wp:Llyn Cerrig Bach]

### Medieval

Caledfwlch in Welsh tales (around 1100 or earlier); Caliburnus in Geoffrey (c. 1136); Escalibor in French verse and in the Vulgate Cycle (c. 1215–1235), where it is also the sword in the stone; separate from the stone sword in the Post-Vulgate and in Malory (1485). Twelfth-century Tuscany has its own sword in a rock, still at the chapel of Montesiepi, attributed to the knight-turned-hermit Galgano Guidotti. [src: wp, wp:Galgano Guidotti]

### Early modern

With the romances, Excalibur faded from fashion after the sixteenth century, though Malory went on being reprinted and read. [src: wp]

### Modern

Tennyson's "Morte d'Arthur" (1842), later part of *Idylls of the King*, gave the English-speaking world its picture of the sword "Excalibur, / Rich in the grace of a king". T. H. White's *The Sword in the Stone* (1938) and Disney's film of it (1963) fixed the stone in popular memory; John Boorman's *Excalibur* (1981) named a whole film after it. [src: idylls, wp]

## Today

- **Film and television:** *The Sword in the Stone* (1963), *Excalibur* (1981), *Monty Python and the Holy Grail* (1975) with its "strange women lying in ponds distributing swords", *King Arthur: Legend of the Sword* (2017), and many series.
- **Language and names:** the name stands for an unbeatable weapon or a sign of rightful power, and is borrowed by products, software, military equipment and roller coasters.
- **Places:** lakes in Wales and Cornwall (Dozmary Pool, Llyn Llydaw and others) are each said locally to be where the sword was returned.
- **Games and fantasy:** a staple of role-playing and video games and of Arthurian fantasy novels.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Medieval: a gift and a trust

In the romances the sword is not Arthur's property but a loan. It comes from the Otherworld (the Lake, Avalon) and must go back there; Arthur's reign is legitimate while he holds it rightly. Merlin's remark that the scabbard is worth more than the sword teaches that protection matters more than conquest. [src: malory1]

### Victorian: the sword of the ideal king

Tennyson made Excalibur the emblem of Arthur's ideal Christian kingship; when the Round Table fails, the sword returns to the mystery it came from. [src: idylls]

### Symbolic and psychological readings

Readers in the Jungian tradition see the sword drawn from stone as consciousness and will emerging from matter, and the sword returned to the water as a life's power given back to the unconscious at death. These are interpretations, not claims about the medieval authors' intentions. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Is the sword in the stone Excalibur?

- **The same sword.** In Robert de Boron and the Vulgate *Merlin*, the sword Arthur draws from the stone is Excalibur. [src: wp]
- **Two swords.** In the Post-Vulgate and Malory, the stone sword breaks and Excalibur comes later from the Lady of the Lake. [src: malory1]
- **Merged in modern memory.** Film and fiction usually blend the two scenes into one sword. [src: wp]

### Where does the name come from?

- **Welsh *Caledfwlch*.** The Welsh name came first, and Geoffrey Latinised it. [src: wp]
- **A Celtic sword-name shared with Ireland.** It is often compared with *Caladbolg*, the sword of the Irish hero Fergus mac Róich. Whether one borrowed from the other, or both come from an older word, is debated. [src: wp, wp:Caladbolg]
- **Latin influence.** Geoffrey's *Caliburnus* may also echo Latin *chalybs*, "steel". [src: wp]

### Did a real custom lie behind the lake?

- **Yes, a memory of votive offerings.** Archaeologists have found many weapons deliberately placed in water across prehistoric Britain and Europe. [src: wp:Llyn Cerrig Bach]
- **Not necessarily.** The scene can be explained as literary invention, centuries after such offerings ended; the link is suggestive, not proven. [src: wp]

## Article

Most swords in legend are famous for what they kill. Excalibur is famous for where it comes from and where it goes. It rises out of a lake in a woman's hand, and in the end a hand takes it back. Between those two moments lies a whole reign. The sword is less a weapon than a lease on power, granted by the Otherworld and returned when the time is up.

That is why the most memorable Excalibur scene is not a battle but a hesitation. Dying, Arthur asks Bedivere to throw the sword into the water, and Bedivere cannot do it. He hides it, lies, hides it again. The best of swords seems too precious to waste. Only when Arthur rebukes him does he throw it, and the lake answers. The scene is about letting go, and it gives grief a gesture: the king has to give back the thing that made him king.

The sword in the stone tells the opposite story, about taking hold. Nobody can draw it but the true king, a boy no one expected. The test cannot be cheated, bought or forced. Later storytellers could not resist joining the two scenes, so that one sword stands for both the claim and its surrender. In Malory they are separate, and the separation matters: the stone sword proves who Arthur is; Excalibur, from the lake, is what he is given to do.

Excalibur is also older than its stories. Behind it stand a Welsh *Caledfwlch*, an Irish *Caladbolg*, and the thousands of real swords that people in prehistoric Europe broke, bent or laid in lakes and rivers. Whether or not the romances remember those offerings, they share the same intuition: that the finest things belong in the end to the waters and the gods, not to us.

For a storyteller, Excalibur is a model of how to give an object a life: an origin that is a gift, a rule that binds the owner, a theft (Morgan's taking of the scabbard), and a return.
