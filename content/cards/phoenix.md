## Overview

A legendary bird that lives for centuries and is reborn from its own death: in the best-known version, it builds a nest of spices, bursts into flame and rises young again from the ashes. Known from Greek writers, linked with the Egyptian sun-bird of Heliopolis, adopted by early Christians as an image of the Resurrection, it remains the world's favourite image of renewal after disaster. [src: wp, herodotus]

## Myth & Origin

Herodotus, around 440 BCE, reports what the priests of Heliopolis in Egypt told him: a sacred bird, red and gold, like an eagle in shape, comes only once every five hundred years, when its father dies. It forms an egg of myrrh, places its father's body inside, and carries it to the temple of the Sun. Herodotus adds that he does not believe it. [src: herodotus]

Ovid's *Metamorphoses* gives the version that became classic in Rome: the phoenix lives five hundred years, builds a nest of cassia, nard, cinnamon and myrrh at the top of a palm, and dies in it; from its body a young phoenix is born, which carries the nest to the temple of the Sun at Heliopolis. Later writers added fire: the bird is consumed in flames and reborn from its ashes. [src: ovid, wp]

The Greeks seem to have connected their bird with the Egyptian *bennu*, a heron sacred to the sun god at Heliopolis, associated with the Benben stone and with creation and renewal. [src: wp]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

Hesiod mentions the phoenix's long life; Herodotus (5th century BCE) tells its story; Ovid, Pliny the Elder and Tacitus repeat and vary it. Roman coins use the phoenix as a sign of the renewal of the age under a new emperor. Around 96 CE the Christian letter known as *1 Clement* uses the phoenix as a proof from nature of the Resurrection. [src: wp]

### Medieval

The *Physiologus* and the medieval bestiaries made the phoenix a type of Christ, who died and rose on the third day. The Old English poem *The Phoenix* retells Lactantius's Latin poem on the bird as a Christian allegory. [src: wp]

### Early modern

Emblem books used the phoenix for uniqueness, chastity and eternal fame; Queen Elizabeth I was painted wearing a phoenix jewel. Alchemists made it the last stage of the Great Work. [src: wp]

### Modern

Cities rebuilt after disaster took it as their emblem: Atlanta after the Civil War burning of 1864; Phoenix, Arizona, founded in 1868 on the ruins of an ancient Hohokam settlement. It flies through modern fantasy, from E. Nesbit's *The Phoenix and the Carpet* (1904) to Fawkes in Harry Potter. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Language:** "to rise like a phoenix from the ashes" means recovering after ruin.
- **Places and emblems:** the city of Phoenix, Arizona; the seal of Atlanta; many companies, schools and teams.
- **Fiction and games:** a favourite creature of fantasy, comics and video games, often with healing tears or rebirth powers.
- **Tattoos:** one of the most popular symbols of survival and new beginnings.
- **A different bird:** the Chinese *fenghuang*, often called the "Chinese phoenix" in English, is a separate creature with its own symbolism (harmony, the empress).
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Greek and Roman

A marvel of nature, a creature without a mate that is its own heir, and a sign of the cycle of the ages. [src: herodotus, ovid]

### Early Christian

A proof from nature that resurrection is possible, and an image of Christ. [src: wp]

### Alchemical

The final stage of the Work, the red stone reborn from fire. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Is the phoenix Egyptian?

- **Yes, the bennu.** Greek writers placed it at Heliopolis, and the Egyptian heron-bird of the sun had similar meanings of renewal. [src: wp]
- **A Greek creation.** The fire, the five hundred years and the nest of spices have no clear Egyptian source; the Greeks built a new myth on Egyptian hints. [src: wp]

### Fire or no fire?

- **No fire in the earliest versions.** Herodotus and Ovid do not burn the bird. [src: herodotus, ovid]
- **Fire later.** Burning and rebirth from ashes come into the story in Roman and Christian times and became the standard version. [src: wp]

## Article

The phoenix is a bird with no parents and no children: it is its own heir. Each one dies so that the next can be born from it, and so in a sense there is only ever one phoenix, living forever by dying again and again. It is one of the oldest and most perfect images of renewal that humans have imagined.

Herodotus, who first tells its story at length, did not believe it. The priests at Heliopolis showed him a picture and told him the bird came from Arabia every five hundred years carrying its dead father in an egg of myrrh. The story is less about fire than about devotion: a child carrying a parent to the temple of the Sun. Ovid gives it a nest of spices on a palm tree, a death, and a birth from the body. Only later does the fire arrive, and with it the image everyone knows.

Early Christians saw at once what the phoenix could mean. A bird that dies and returns on its own was a picture of the Resurrection drawn by nature. In the medieval bestiaries it stands beside the pelican as a figure of Christ.

The modern world has used the phoenix above all for survival. Cities that burned (Atlanta), cities built on ancient ruins (Phoenix), people recovering from illness or loss: all have taken the bird as their sign. The phoenix does not promise that nothing will burn. It promises that something can rise from what did.

For a storyteller, the phoenix is the shape of every comeback: not avoiding destruction, but passing through it and becoming new.
