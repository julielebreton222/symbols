## Overview

A cross with its arms bent at right angles: one of the oldest and most widespread signs in the world. In Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism it has been a sacred symbol of good fortune and well-being for thousands of years, and it still is, seen on temples, doorways and in daily worship across Asia. In the West it is overshadowed by its use as the emblem of Nazi Germany, which has made it a symbol of hatred and genocide. Both facts must be held together. [src: wp]

## Myth & Origin

The Sanskrit word *svastika* comes from *su* (good) and *asti* (it is): "that which is conducive to well-being". In Hindu tradition it marks auspicious beginnings: drawn in red powder on thresholds, account books and wedding invitations, and associated with Ganesha, remover of obstacles, and with the sun and the four directions. In Jainism it is one of the eight auspicious symbols, and its four arms stand for the four states of existence through which souls are reborn (gods, humans, animals and plants, and hell-beings). In Buddhism it marks the Buddha's footprints and heart, and in East Asia the character 卍 (*manji*) marks Buddhist temples on maps. [src: wp]

The shape itself is far older than any of these explanations. It appears on prehistoric artefacts in Europe and Asia, on seals of the Indus Valley Civilisation (c. 2500 BCE), on Greek pottery of the Geometric period, on Celtic, Germanic and Anglo-Saxon objects, in Roman mosaics, in Byzantine and medieval churches, and in the art of many Native American peoples. [src: wp]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

A near-universal ornament and sacred sign, from Neolithic Europe and the Indus Valley to Greece, Rome, China and the Americas. In India it becomes a sign of blessing in Vedic and later Hindu, Buddhist and Jain practice. [src: wp]

### Medieval

Sacred across South, Central and East Asia; in Europe a decorative motif (the "fylfot" in English heraldry, the "gammadion" in Byzantine art) on church floors and textiles. [src: wp]

### Early modern

Continuous use in Asian religions; in Europe, mostly ornamental. [src: wp]

### Modern

Heinrich Schliemann's finds of swastikas at Troy in the 1870s, and nineteenth-century racial theories about an "Aryan" people, led European nationalists to adopt it as a supposed sign of Aryan heritage. Before 1920 it was also a popular Western good-luck charm, used by scouts, companies and sports teams. The Nazi Party adopted a black, tilted, right-facing swastika in 1920, and it became the centre of Germany's national flag (1935–1945). After the Holocaust and the Second World War its public display was banned in Germany and some other countries. In Asia it remained an everyday sacred sign. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Living faith:** for over a billion Hindus, Buddhists and Jains it remains sacred: drawn at the doorway during Diwali, painted on vehicles and shops, used in weddings and housewarmings, carved on temples. To believers it means auspiciousness, blessing, the eternal, and (for Jains) the cycle of rebirth they seek to escape.
- **East Asia:** the 卍 manji marks temples on Japanese and Korean maps and appears on Buddhist objects.
- **In the West:** primarily known as the Nazi emblem; displaying it is banned or restricted in several countries, usually with exceptions for religious and historical use.
- **Hate symbol:** still used by neo-Nazi and white-supremacist groups.
- **Reclaiming:** Hindu, Buddhist and Jain organisations campaign to explain its sacred meaning and to distinguish it from the Nazi symbol.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Hindu

A sign of well-being and good fortune, of the sun's movement and the four directions, and of Ganesha. [src: wp]

### Jain

The four arms as the four destinies of the soul; the symbol on the Jain emblem reminds believers of the goal of liberation. [src: wp]

### Buddhist

A sign of the Buddha's heart and mind, of eternity and good fortune. [src: wp]

### Nazi ideology

A pseudo-historical "Aryan" sign turned into the emblem of a racist, genocidal regime. Germans called it *Hakenkreuz*, "hooked cross". [src: wp]

### After 1945, in the West

A symbol of the Holocaust, of hatred and of the crimes of Nazism; for Jews and many others it can still cause great pain. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Can the sacred swastika be "reclaimed" in the West?

- **Yes, with education.** Hindu, Buddhist and Jain groups argue that their ancient sacred symbol should not be erased because of its theft by the Nazis, and some laws now distinguish religious use from Nazi use. [src: wp]
- **With great care.** Jewish organisations and others note how deeply the symbol is tied to the Holocaust in Western eyes, and ask for sensitivity in public display outside religious contexts. [src: wp]

### Should the Nazi symbol be called a "swastika"?

- **Use "hooked cross" or *Hakenkreuz*.** Some Hindu and Jain groups and scholars propose this, since Hitler used the German term, to separate the hate symbol from the sacred sign. [src: wp]
- **"Swastika" is the established name.** Others note that "swastika" is the standard English word for both, and changing usage is slow. [src: wp]

### Does the direction matter?

- **Sometimes.** Some traditions distinguish right-facing and left-facing forms (*sauwastika*), and the Nazi version is always right-facing and tilted. [src: wp]
- **Not consistently.** Both directions appear in Hindu, Buddhist and Jain art, and direction alone does not tell sacred from Nazi use. [src: wp]

## Article

The swastika is the hardest symbol in this book, because it means two opposite things at once, and both are true. For thousands of years, and for hundreds of millions of people today, it is a sign of blessing: a little red mark drawn at a doorway at Diwali, painted on a new car, printed on a wedding card, carved on a temple. Its name means "it is well". For many others, especially in Europe, North America and among Jews everywhere, it is the face of the Holocaust.

The shape is ancient and almost universal. It appears on pottery, textiles and stones from prehistoric Europe to the Indus Valley, from Greece to China to the American Southwest. Perhaps its spinning arms recalled the sun, the turning of the sky, or the four directions. In India it became sacred to three great religions, each of which gave it its own meaning.

Then, in the nineteenth century, European scholars and nationalists, looking for the symbols of an imagined Aryan race, seized upon it. Schliemann found it at Troy; racial theorists drew lines from Troy to Germany. In 1920 the Nazi Party made it its emblem, and within twenty-five years it flew over the murder of six million Jews and millions of others. In the West it has never recovered.

Hindus, Buddhists and Jains have not stopped using it, and should not have to. Today many campaign to explain its true meaning and to separate it from the Nazi "hooked cross". Others ask that the pain it carries be respected. The swastika is a lesson in how a symbol can be stolen, and in how its rightful owners and its victims may both deserve to be heard.

For a writer, it is a hard but important reminder that symbols have no fixed meaning outside the people who use them, and that the same sign can be a blessing on one street and a threat on the next.
