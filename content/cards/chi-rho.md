## Overview

One of the oldest Christian symbols: the first two letters of *Christos* in Greek, Χ (chi) and Ρ (rho), laid over each other. It became famous when the Roman emperor Constantine put it on his soldiers' shields and his battle standard in 312, and it remains a common sign of Christ on altars, vestments and church art. [src: wp]

## Myth & Origin

In 312 Constantine marched on Rome against his rival Maxentius. The Christian writer Lactantius, writing soon after, says Constantine was told in a dream to mark the heavenly sign of God on his soldiers' shields, and did so with a letter X turned and bent at the top: a Chi-Rho. Eusebius of Caesarea, in his *Life of Constantine* written decades later, says the emperor told him on oath that he saw a cross of light in the sky with the words "In this sign, conquer" (Greek *en toutō nika*, Latin *in hoc signo vinces*), and then dreamed that Christ told him to make a standard of it. Constantine won the battle at the Milvian Bridge, and soon afterwards Christianity became a tolerated, then favoured, religion of the Empire. [src: wp]

The standard, called the *labarum*, carried the Chi-Rho at its top. [src: wp]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

The combined letters were already used in Greek writing as an abbreviation (for example for *chrēstos*, "good", in the margins of texts) and appear on some earlier coins. From 312 on, the Chi-Rho appears on Constantine's coins, helmets and standards, on sarcophagi, in catacomb paintings and in mosaics, such as the fourth-century Hinton St Mary mosaic in Roman Britain, which places it behind the head of Christ. Often it is flanked by Alpha and Omega. [src: wp]

### Medieval

The monogram stayed common in Christian art; the great "Chi-Rho page" of the Book of Kells (c. 800) turns the opening of Christ's birth in Matthew's Gospel into a blaze of ornament around the letters XP. [src: wp]

### Early modern

Catholic and Orthodox liturgical art kept it, alongside the IHS monogram, on vestments, altars and chalices. [src: wp]

### Modern

It is still used by many churches: on altar cloths, vestments and the Easter candle, in stained glass and on gravestones; it is encoded in Unicode as ☧. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Worship:** embroidered on vestments and altar cloths, carved on altars and fonts, painted on the Paschal (Easter) candle. For Christians it is a sign of Christ himself, of his victory over death, and of the faith of the early Church.
- **Institutions:** in the emblems of churches, schools and chaplaincies.
- **History and heritage:** on Roman coins, mosaics and manuscripts in museums.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Constantine and the early Church

A sign of victory: the God of the Christians granting triumph to the emperor who honoured him. [src: wp]

### Liturgical

A monogram of Christ's name, a way of making the name present on objects of worship. [src: wp]

### Modern historians

A turning point in the alliance of Christianity and imperial power, viewed by some with admiration and by others with criticism. [src: wp]

## Debates

### What did Constantine see?

- **A vision in the sky.** Eusebius reports the emperor's own account of a heavenly sign. [src: wp]
- **A dream.** Lactantius, the earlier writer, speaks only of a dream. [src: wp]
- **A natural event, or a later story.** Historians have suggested a solar halo, or a story shaped after the victory; some note an earlier report of a vision of Apollo in 310. [src: wp]

### Was it already a Christian sign?

- **Christian from 312.** It became a Christian symbol through Constantine. [src: wp]
- **Earlier use.** The combined letters were already a Greek abbreviation and appear in non-Christian contexts, so Constantine adopted an existing form and gave it a new meaning. [src: wp]

## Article

The Chi-Rho is a name made into a picture. Take the first two Greek letters of Christ's title, X and P, and lay one over the other: the result looks like a cross with a loop, a key, a standard. It is so simple that it may have been used by scribes long before anyone thought of it as holy.

Its fame came from a battlefield. In 312 an ambitious general, Constantine, was about to fight for Rome. According to the Christian authors who told the story, he received a sign from heaven, painted it on his men's shields and won. Within a year Christianity was legal in the Empire; within a century it was the Empire's religion. The little monogram rode at the head of the imperial army on the labarum, and it is no exaggeration to say it marks one of the hinges of Western history.

Historians still argue about what Constantine saw, and what he believed. But the symbol outlived the arguments. It moved from shields to tombs, from coins to mosaics, from the margins of Gospel books to the huge illuminated page of the Book of Kells, where the two letters swell into an entire world of spirals, cats, mice and angels.

Today it is quieter: stitched on a priest's stole, carved on an altar, painted on the Easter candle. For believers it still says what it said in the catacombs: Christ is here.

For a storyteller, the Chi-Rho shows how a vision can be both a private experience and a public weapon, and how a sign born in war can end up as a sign of peace.
