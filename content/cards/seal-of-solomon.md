## Overview

The magic signet ring of King Solomon, engraved with a divine name or a star, which gave him power over demons and jinn and, in Islamic tradition, the speech of animals and command of the wind. The star on it, drawn as a hexagram or a pentagram, became one of the most widespread protective signs of Jewish, Christian and Islamic magic, and appears on old coins and flags of the Muslim world. [src: wp]

## Myth & Origin

The Bible praises Solomon's wisdom but gives him no magic ring. That came later. In the *Testament of Solomon*, a Greek text from the early centuries CE, the archangel Michael brings Solomon a ring engraved with a seal, with which he binds demons and makes them build the Temple. [src: wp]

The Babylonian Talmud tells how Solomon captured the demon king Ashmedai (Asmodeus) with a chain and a ring bearing God's name, to learn where to find the *shamir*, a creature that could cut stone for the Temple. Ashmedai tricked Solomon into giving him the ring, threw it away, flung the king hundreds of miles and took his place; Solomon wandered as a beggar until he found the ring again (in some later versions, in the belly of a fish). [src: wp]

Islamic tradition made Sulayman (Solomon) a prophet who ruled over humans, jinn, animals and the wind; the Qur'an tells how the jinn worked for him. Later stories gave him a ring from heaven on which God's greatest name was engraved. In the *Arabian Nights*, a fisherman finds a copper bottle sealed with the seal of Sulayman, from which a jinni escapes. [src: quran, nights]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

The *Testament of Solomon* and Jewish magical texts make Solomon the master of demons; amulets of late antiquity show him on horseback spearing a demon. [src: wp]

### Medieval

In Jewish, Christian and Islamic magic the seal (hexagram or pentagram) was carved on amulets, bowls and talismans. Islamic art used it on coins, tiles, metalwork and banners. European grimoires such as the *Lesser Key of Solomon* drew on the legend. [src: wp]

### Early modern

The seal decorated buildings across the Ottoman world and North Africa; European occultists made "Solomon's seals" central to ceremonial magic. [src: wp]

### Modern

A pentagram called the Seal of Solomon appears on the flag of Morocco (since 1915) and appeared on earlier flags and coins in the region; the hexagram as a Jewish emblem became the Star of David. The plant called "Solomon's seal" (*Polygonatum*) takes its name from marks on its root. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Faith and folk belief:** Solomon (Sulayman) is honoured as a wise king in Judaism and Christianity and as a prophet in Islam; the seal survives in protective amulets in parts of the Muslim and Jewish worlds.
- **Flags and emblems:** the green pentagram of Morocco; historic coins and seals.
- **Esotericism and fantasy:** grimoires, occult orders, novels and games (for example Jonathan Stroud's *Bartimaeus* trilogy).
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Jewish

The power of God's name, which even the king of demons must obey; and a warning, in the Ashmedai story, that a king can lose everything when he trusts his power too much. [src: wp]

### Islamic

A sign of the authority God gave to Sulayman over all creatures, visible and invisible. [src: quran]

### Magical and esoteric

A master key to binding spirits, much copied in amulets and grimoires. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Hexagram or pentagram?

- **Both.** Medieval sources draw the seal as either; usage differs by region and period. [src: wp]
- **Distinct today.** The hexagram is now usually the Star of David, while the pentagram dominates in Islamic and magical use. [src: wp]

### Real history or legend?

- **Legend.** No ring of Solomon is known from the Bible or archaeology; the ring belongs to later Jewish, Christian and Islamic lore. [src: wp]

## Article

The Seal of Solomon is a story about how wisdom became magic. The Bible's Solomon is a wise judge, a builder, a poet and a king who prays for understanding. Within a few centuries, Jews, Christians and Muslims were telling of a ring that let him command demons, understand the speech of birds and ants, and make the jinn build his Temple.

The ring's power comes from a name, the hidden name of God engraved on it. That is a deep idea in all three traditions: words, and above all the divine name, have power over the world. Demons can resist armies, but not the name.

The best stories about the ring are about losing it. The Talmud tells how Ashmedai, king of demons, tricked Solomon out of his ring and sat on his throne in his shape, while the true king wandered as a beggar saying "I am Solomon" to people who laughed. It is a story of pride and humility: the wisest king is nothing without the gift he was given.

The seal's star outlived the ring. As a hexagram it became the Star of David; as a pentagram it flies on the flag of Morocco; on amulets it still guards houses and children. The *Arabian Nights* added the bottle and the jinni, and gave the modern world its genie.

For a storyteller, the seal is an object of command, and a reminder that command can be stolen.
