## Overview

The understanding that animals, plants, rivers, mountains, stones, weather and made things can be alive with spirit, or are themselves persons to be greeted, thanked and bargained with. "Animism" is a scholars' word, not the name of any one religion: people who live this way usually speak of their own kami, orishas, manitous, ancestors or Dreaming, not of "animism". In Mexico, it can be seen every autumn when the monarch butterflies arrive around the Day of the Dead and are welcomed as the souls of the departed. [src: wp, wp:Mazahua people]

## Myth & Origin

The word comes from Latin *anima*, "breath, soul". The English anthropologist Edward Burnett Tylor made it a key term in *Primitive Culture* (1871), proposing as the "minimum definition of Religion, the belief in Spiritual Beings", and arguing that every religion grew out of the idea that dreams, breath and shadows reveal a soul that can leave the body and also lives in animals, trees and things. [src: tylor, wp]

Tylor placed animism at the bottom of a ladder that led up to monotheism and then science. That ladder has been abandoned, and critics point out that the "old animism" assumed animists could not tell persons from things, a view they call colonial. [src: wp]

The "new animism" began with the anthropologist A. Irving Hallowell's work among the Ojibwe (Anishinaabe) of Canada in the mid-twentieth century. For the people he knew, a person did not have to be human: there were rock persons and bear persons, and one learned to be a person by treating them with respect. Nurit Bird-David (1999) and the religious-studies scholar Graham Harvey built on this. In Harvey's words the term is still valuable for "a style of religious and cultural relating to the world": not a belief about hidden spirits, but a way of living among many kinds of persons, only some of them human. [src: wp]

Long before the word, Europe had its own spirits of place. The Romans gave every place a *genius loci*; James George Frazer gathered in *The Golden Bough* (1890) the tree spirits, sacred groves and harvest spirits of Europe; and W. Y. Evans-Wentz recorded in *The Fairy-Faith in Celtic Countries* (1911) the living Irish, Scottish, Welsh, Manx and Breton belief in fairy places and beings. [src: frazer, wentz, wp:Genius loci]

## Under this umbrella

### Mexico: the dead come back as wings

- **[[monarch-butterfly|Monarch butterfly]].** Each autumn millions of monarchs fly from Canada and the United States to the high oyamel fir forests of Michoacán and the State of Mexico, arriving around 1–2 November. The Mazahua believe the souls of the departed return on the Day of the Dead as monarchs, to enjoy the fruit and bread left on the altars; they welcome them with a procession from church to cemetery, and say goodbye with one back. For the Purépecha of Michoacán, the butterflies on their way to the winter forests are the spirits of the dead journeying from the afterlife. [src: wp:Mazahua people, wp:Ghosts in Mexican culture, wp:Monarch Butterfly Biosphere Reserve]
- **[[black-witch-moth|Black witch moth]] (*Ascalapha odorata*).** A huge dark moth that flies into houses at night. In Mexico it is the *mariposa de la muerte*, "butterfly of death"; in Nahuatl *miquipapalotl* ("death moth"); in Yucatec Maya *x-mahan-nah*, "the one who borrows a house". If it enters a house where someone is sick, the sick person will die; in the lower Rio Grande Valley, only if it visits all four corners. Moths have been tied to death and the number four in Mesoamerica since before the Spanish conquest. Elsewhere its meaning turns: in Jamaica it is the *duppy bat*, a soul not at rest; in Hawaii the soul of someone just dead come to say goodbye; in the Bahamas and South Texas a "money moth" that brings luck. [src: wp:Ascalapha odorata]
- **[[butterfly|Butterflies]] of fire and war.** In ancient central Mexico some butterflies were the reborn souls of dead warriors, often shown with a jaguar's jaws; the goddess Itzpapalotl is the "obsidian butterfly". [src: wp:Butterfly]
- **[[hummingbird|Hummingbird]].** Aztec warriors who died in battle and women who died in childbirth followed the sun across the sky and then returned as hummingbirds. The war and sun god Huitzilopochtli's name means "hummingbird of the south" (or "left"); the Dominican friar Diego Durán described the hummingbird as seeming to die in winter and being "reborn" in spring. [src: wp:Ghosts in Mexican culture, wp:Huitzilopochtli]
- **[[owl|Owl]] (*tecolote*).** For the Aztecs and the Maya, a messenger of death: Mictlantecuhtli, lord of the underworld, appears with owls; the *Popol Vuh* makes owls the messengers of Xibalba. A Mexican saying still runs *Cuando el tecolote canta, el indio muere*, "when the owl sings, the Indian dies". [src: wp:Owl]
- **[[xoloitzcuintle|Xoloitzcuintle]].** The hairless dog named after the god Xolotl. Dogs like it were buried with their owners to guide their souls on the journey to the underworld. [src: wp:Xoloitzcuintle]
- **[[cempasuchil|Cempasúchil]] (Mexican marigold).** From Nahuatl *cempōhualxōchitl*, "twenty flower", twenty being a full count. Its bright petals and strong scent are thought to guide souls from the cemeteries to their families' homes on the Day of the Dead. [src: wp:Tagetes erecta, wp:Day of the Dead]
- **[[ofrenda|Ofrenda]], [[copal|copal]], [[calavera|calaveras]] and [[papel-picado|papel picado]].** The altar of the [[day-of-the-dead|Day of the Dead]] with the dead's favourite food and drink, the fragrant tree resin burned for them, the sugar and clay skulls, and cut paper banners. [src: wp:Day of the Dead]
- **[[nagual|Nagual]] (*nahual*).** In Mesoamerican folk religion, a person who can turn into their *tonal*, the animal counterpart that shares their life and fate; birth dates in the ritual calendar can show who has this gift. [src: wp:Nagual]
- **[[alebrije|Alebrije]].** Fantastic many-animal creatures first made in papier-mâché by Pedro Linares in Mexico City in 1936, after he saw them in a fever dream; Oaxacan carvers later took them up in copal wood. A modern folk art, now often treated as spirit guides. [src: wp:Alebrije]
- **[[chaneque|Chaneques]] and [[alux|aluxes]].** Small guardian spirits of forest, water and field, the chaneques of central and Gulf Mexico, the aluxes of the Yucatec Maya, who must be given offerings or they make mischief.
- **[[ceiba|Ceiba]] and [[cenote|cenote]].** The great ceiba tree is the Maya [[mesoamerican-world-tree|world tree]] that joins underworld, earth and sky; lineage ancestors were said to have emerged from its roots. Cenotes, the water-filled sinkholes of Yucatán, were doorways to the underworld. [src: wp:Ghosts in Mexican culture]
- **[[jaguar|Jaguar]], [[quetzal|quetzal]], [[axolotl|axolotl]], [[coyote|coyote]] and the [[eagle-and-serpent|eagle on the cactus]].** The jaguar of night and rulers, the quetzal whose green plumes were worth more than gold, the axolotl named for Xolotl, the trickster coyote, and the eagle eating a serpent that showed the Mexica where to found their city and now stands on Mexico's flag.

### Japan: kami everywhere

- **[[kami|Kami]].** In Shinto the sacred is present in mountains, waterfalls, boulders, trees, the sun, thunder, echoes, foxes, rice fields and the ancestors. A kami is honoured in a [[shintai|shintai]] (a body for the kami: a mountain, a stone, a mirror) or called into a [[yorishiro|yorishiro]], and holy trees and rocks are tied with the rice-straw rope [[shimenawa|shimenawa]] and approached through a [[torii|torii]]. [src: wp:Kami]
- **[[kodama|Kodama]], [[kitsune|kitsune]] and [[tsukumogami|tsukumogami]].** The spirits of old trees, the fox spirits, and the household tools that come alive after a hundred years.

### The Americas, north

- **[[totem|Totem]] and [[totem-pole|totem pole]].** The word comes from the Ojibwe *doodem*, a person's clan and its animal (crane, loon, bear, marten); totem poles of the Pacific Northwest Coast tell the crests and histories of families. [src: wp:Totem]
- **[[manitou|Manitou]].** In Anishinaabe and other Algonquian traditions, the spiritual power present in beings and places.
- **[[raven-in-native-american-tradition|Raven]], [[thunderbird|Thunderbird]], [[bear|bear]], [[wolf|wolf]], [[salmon-of-knowledge|salmon]] and [[eagle|eagle]].** Raven the trickster who stole the light; the Thunderbird whose wings make thunder; the bear as a grandfather; the eagle feather as a gift that must be honoured.
- **[[white-buffalo|White buffalo]].** For the Lakota, the sign of White Buffalo Calf Woman, who brought the sacred pipe; the birth of a white calf is still greeted as a holy event.
- **[[inuksuk|Inuksuk]], [[dreamcatcher|dreamcatcher]], [[medicine-wheel|medicine wheel]], [[power-animal|power animal]].** Stone figures that speak for people on the Arctic land; the web that catches bad dreams; the stone circles of the Plains; the guardian animal of the shaman's journey.

### Africa

- **[[iroko|Iroko]], [[baobab|baobab]] and the [[osun-osogbo-sacred-grove|Osun-Osogbo Sacred Grove]].** Trees and groves where orishas and ancestors live; the grove of the river goddess Ọṣun in Nigeria is a World Heritage Site.
- **[[mami-wata|Mami Wata]], [[nkisi|nkisi]] and [[adinkra-symbols|Adinkra]].** The water spirit of West and Central Africa; the Kongo power figures that house a spirit; the Akan signs that carry proverbs. Among the Ndembu of Zambia, Victor Turner found the [[mukula-tree|mukula]] and [[milk-tree|milk tree]] standing for blood and mother's milk, and the [[makishi-masked-dancers|makishi]] masked dancers as returning ancestors.

### Oceania and Australia

- **[[taniwha|Taniwha]] and the [[whanganui-river|Whanganui River]].** Māori guardian beings of rivers and the sea; in 2017 New Zealand made the Whanganui River a legal person, with the rights and duties of one, in a settlement with the iwi who call it their ancestor. [src: wp:Whanganui River]
- **[[the-dreaming|The Dreaming]], [[rainbow-serpent|Rainbow Serpent]], [[uluru|Uluru]] and [[tjurunga|tjurunga]].** In Aboriginal Australian traditions, the ancestral beings shaped the land and remain in it; country itself is alive and sacred.

### Europe and the north

- **[[sacred-grove|Sacred groves]], [[holy-well|holy wells]], [[clootie-well|clootie wells]] and [[fairy-tree|fairy trees]].** Places with spirits, where rags are still tied and lone hawthorns are not cut. [src: wentz]
- **[[yggdrasil|Yggdrasil]], [[irminsul|Irminsul]], [[sacred-tree-at-uppsala|the tree at Uppsala]], [[oak|oak]], [[mistletoe|mistletoe]], [[green-man|Green Man]].** Holy trees and the face in the leaves. [src: frazer]
- **[[domovoi|Domovoi]], [[genius-loci|genius loci]], [[sami-drum|Sámi drum]].** The Slavic house spirit fed behind the stove; the Roman spirit of each place; the drum that carried the Sámi noaidi between worlds.

### Moths and butterflies elsewhere

- **The soul with wings.** The Greek word for butterfly, *psychē*, also means "soul"; a Roman relief shows a butterfly leaving a dead man's mouth; in Japan, Lafcadio Hearn reported, a butterfly could be the soul of the living or the dead. The [[deaths-head-hawkmoth|death's-head hawkmoth]], with its skull pattern, was a European omen of death. [src: wp:Butterfly]
- **And:** [[cicada|cicada]] (rebirth from the earth), [[firefly|firefly]] (souls of the dead over the water in Japan), [[dragonfly|dragonfly]], [[bee|bee]], [[spider|spider]], [[frog|frog]], [[serpent|serpent]], [[fox|fox]], [[hare|hare]], [[stag|stag]], [[raven|raven]], [[crane|crane]], [[swan|swan]], [[whale|whale]], [[turtle|turtle]], and the elements: [[mountain|mountains]], [[river|rivers]], [[stone|stones]], [[fire|fire]], [[wind|wind]], [[rain|rain]], [[sun|sun]] and [[moon|moon]]. Each of them can be found on the Animism theme page.

## Lifetime

### Ancient

Most of the world's ways of living with spirits are older than writing. The Kojiki (712) records the kami of Japan; Roman religion gave each place its genius; the Aztec and Maya codices and later Spanish chronicles describe souls returning as birds and butterflies and dogs leading the dead. [src: wp:Kami, wp:Genius loci, wp:Ghosts in Mexican culture]

### Medieval

Christian and Muslim authorities condemned the worship of trees, springs and stones again and again, which shows how long it lasted; many holy wells and sacred trees were simply given to saints. [src: frazer]

### Early modern

Missionaries and colonial officials across the Americas, Africa and Asia described local spirits as idols or devils. In Mexico, Nahua and Maya practices blended with Catholic All Saints' and All Souls' Days into the Day of the Dead. [src: wp:Day of the Dead]

### Modern

Tylor named animism (1871); Frazer and Evans-Wentz collected the European evidence; Hallowell, Bird-David, Harvey and the French anthropologist Philippe Descola turned it from "a mistake" into "a way of relating". Today rivers and forests receive legal personhood, and the monarch's winter forests became a World Heritage Site (2008). [src: wp, wp:Whanganui River, wp:Monarch Butterfly Biosphere Reserve]

## Today

- **In living faiths:** Shinto shrines, Yoruba and Akan worship of orishas and abosom, the Anishinaabe and Lakota ceremonies, and the Aboriginal Australians' care for country are living religions; for those who practise them the spirits are present and respect for them is a duty, not a symbol.
- **In Mexico:** each autumn families place cempasúchil, copal and food on ofrendas and greet the monarchs; many still shoo a black witch moth gently out of the house.
- **In law and ecology:** rivers (Whanganui), forests (Te Urewera) and natural features have been granted legal personhood, and environmental thinkers borrow from animist ideas.
- **In Paganism and popular spirituality:** many modern Pagans call themselves animists; "spirit animal" is popular, though Indigenous people often ask that their sacred terms not be used casually.
- **In art and story:** Studio Ghibli's forest spirits, alebrijes and Day of the Dead imagery have carried these ideas around the world.
[src: wp, wp:Whanganui River, wp:Day of the Dead]

## How it was seen

### Victorian anthropology: the first religion

For Tylor, animism was the "minimum" of religion, a reasonable but mistaken theory made by early people from dreams and death, which history would leave behind. [src: tylor]

### The people themselves

Most animist traditions do not see "spirits inside things" at all: the river, the bear or the mountain is someone. Kinship, gifts, permission and gratitude come first; beliefs come second. [src: wp]

### The new animism

Hallowell, Bird-David and Harvey read animism as a way of relating: learning to live well among many kinds of persons, of whom humans are just one kind. [src: wp]

### Psychological

Psychologists (Jean Piaget among them) used "animism" for children's belief that things are alive, and the reference books often read animals and trees as images of the unconscious. See "What the books say" on the linked cards. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Should the word be used at all?

- **Retire it.** The word was invented to rank peoples from "primitive" to "civilised", and it lumps together hundreds of very different religions. [src: wp]
- **Keep it, redefined.** Harvey and others hold that, used carefully, it names a real and respectable way of being in the world. [src: wp]

### Is it a religion?

- **A religion of its own.** Some modern Pagans and others call themselves animists as a faith. [src: wp]
- **A dimension of many religions.** Most scholars treat it as something found within Shinto, Indigenous religions, folk Catholicism and more, rather than a separate faith. [src: wp]

### Do the monarchs carry the souls of the dead?

- **An old belief.** The Mazahua and Purépecha connect the butterflies with the returning dead, and butterflies were warrior souls in Aztec thought. [src: wp:Mazahua people, wp:Butterfly]
- **How old it is, is unclear.** The link between monarchs and the Day of the Dead is reported from living communities; how far back it goes before the conquest is not documented. {unverified}

## Article

Every autumn, across the mountains west of Mexico City, the fir trees turn orange. Millions of monarch butterflies arrive from the north and cling to the branches in such numbers that the boughs bend. They come at the start of November, at the same time families are laying marigolds on the graves and setting out bread and fruit on their altars. For the Mazahua, the butterflies are the dead themselves, coming home to taste the offerings. Then the processions turn round, and the visitors are sent off again.

This is what the dry word "animism" tries to describe. A Victorian scholar looking at it would have said that people "believe butterflies have souls". The people themselves would say something simpler: these are our grandparents, and you greet your grandparents.

The same country has a darker visitor. The black witch moth, as wide as a hand, flies in through an open door at night. In Mexico it is the butterfly of death; in Maya it is "the one who borrows a house". If someone is ill and it visits the four corners of the room, the family knows. Mesoamerica has linked moths with death and with the number four since before the Spanish came. Yet the same moth, a few hundred miles away, means a lost soul in Jamaica, a goodbye from the newly dead in Hawaii, and a lottery win in South Texas. One creature, several worlds.

Around them gathers a whole household of the other-than-human: the owl that sings before a death, the hairless dog who leads the soul across the river to the underworld, the hummingbird that is a fallen warrior come back to the flowers, the marigold whose scent draws the dead home, the nagual who walks out at night as a jaguar. None of them is an idea in a book. They are neighbours, and neighbours have manners.

That is the heart of animism in the "new" sense. It is not a theory about invisible spirits but a set of good manners for living in a crowded world. Ask the tree before you cut it. Thank the deer. Leave rice for the kami of the rock and bread for the dead. Do not cut the lone hawthorn in the field. Bears, rivers and rocks have their own lives and their own claims.

For a storyteller this opens every door. A river can sue. A moth can be a messenger, a thief of houses or a grandmother saying goodbye. A whole season can be a homecoming. The question is never "is it alive?" but "who is it, and what does it want?"
