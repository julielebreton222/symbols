## Overview

A serpent or dragon that swallows its own tail, forming a circle with no beginning or end. From Egyptian tombs to Greek alchemy, Norse myth and modern psychology, it stands for eternity, the cycle of destruction and renewal, and the unity of all things: in the alchemists' words, *hen to pan*, "the All is One". [src: wp]

## Myth & Origin

The oldest known images come from Egypt. On one of the gilded shrines of Tutankhamun (14th century BCE), the *Enigmatic Book of the Netherworld* shows a great figure encircled by serpents biting their tails, guarding the sun god's renewal in the underworld. Egyptian thought imagined the world surrounded by a serpent of the primeval waters. [src: wp]

The Greek name means "tail-devourer". In Greco-Egyptian alchemy of the early centuries CE, the *Chrysopoeia of Cleopatra* draws a serpent swallowing its tail around the words *hen to pan*. Horapollo's *Hieroglyphica* (5th century CE) explains it as the Egyptian sign for the universe. [src: wp]

Similar serpents appear elsewhere: in Norse myth Jörmungandr, the Midgard Serpent, lies in the ocean around the world, biting its own tail. [src: prose]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

Egyptian funerary art (14th century BCE onward); Gnostic and magical papyri and gems of the Roman period; the alchemy of Alexandria, where it became the emblem of the Great Work. [src: wp]

### Medieval

Byzantine and Arabic alchemical manuscripts, and through them Latin ones, keep the tail-biter as a picture of matter's endless cycle. Norse poetry, written down in thirteenth-century Iceland, gives the Midgard Serpent its encircling form. [src: wp, prose]

### Early modern

Alchemical and emblem books of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries made the ouroboros one of their favourite images, often as a winged dragon devouring a wingless one (the volatile and the fixed). It also marked eternity on tombs, clocks and printers' devices. [src: wp]

### Modern

The chemist August Kekulé said, in a speech of 1890, that the ring structure of benzene came to him in a reverie of a snake seizing its own tail. Carl Jung and Erich Neumann made the ouroboros a key image of the undivided, original state of the psyche. It is a favourite in logos, tattoos and fantasy fiction. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Tattoos and jewellery:** among the most popular symbolic tattoos, for cycles, rebirth and infinity.
- **Logos and design:** used for recycling, sustainability and "circular" projects, and by esoteric and occult groups.
- **Science stories:** Kekulé's snake and benzene; the circular economy.
- **Fiction:** fantasy novels, films, video games and anime use it for eternity, immortality or a closed loop of time.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Egyptian

The serpent circling the world and the sun's journey: protection of the cosmos and the daily renewal of time. [src: wp]

### Alchemical

The Great Work as one continuous process in which matter dies and is reborn: "the All is One". The serpent devours itself, and from its own substance makes itself again. [src: wp]

### Gnostic and magical

For some Gnostics the serpent around the world was the boundary of the material cosmos, sometimes a sinister one. [src: wp]

### Jungian and depth psychology

For Jung, a symbol of the self-contained wholeness that the psyche seeks; for Erich Neumann (*The Origins and History of Consciousness*, 1949) the "uroboros" is the earliest stage of consciousness, before the ego separates from the unconscious. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Did Kekulé really dream it?

- **Yes, a famous creative dream.** Kekulé told the story himself in 1890, and it has become a classic tale of the creative unconscious. [src: wp]
- **A later embellishment.** Historians note he told the story twenty-five years after the discovery, and that others had proposed related ideas; the reverie may be partly a performance. [src: wp]

### One symbol or many?

- **A universal archetype.** Jungians see the tail-devourer as appearing independently in many cultures. [src: wp]
- **A traceable history.** Others follow a concrete line from Egypt to Alexandria to European alchemy, and warn against counting every circular serpent as the same symbol. [src: wp]

## Article

The ouroboros is a paradox you can draw in one stroke. A creature eats itself, and instead of disappearing it becomes a perfect circle. Destruction and creation are the same act; the end of the serpent is its beginning. It is perhaps the simplest image humans have made of time that does not run out.

The Egyptians used it to protect the sun. Each night the sun god travels through the underworld, and each dawn he is born again; the serpent coiled around the hidden mysteries keeps chaos out of that cycle. Later, in Alexandria, the alchemists made the same serpent their emblem. For them, matter was alive and always changing; the work was to understand the cycle well enough to guide it. *Hen to pan*, the All is One, they wrote inside the coil.

The circle also hides a darker thought. A thing that feeds on itself can never be fed by anything else. The serpent around the world in Norse myth is a monster, and when it lets go of its tail at Ragnarök the world ends. For some Gnostics, the coil around the cosmos was a prison wall. A loop can be eternity or a trap.

In the modern world the ouroboros lives on as a promise and a warning: recycling and sustainability on one side, vicious circles on the other. Kekulé's dream of a snake turning into the ring of benzene made it a patron of scientific imagination.

For a storyteller, it is the shape of every story that ends where it began, and every character who must consume their old self to become new.
