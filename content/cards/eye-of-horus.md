## Overview

The *wedjat*, "the sound (whole) one": a stylised human eye with the markings of a falcon's cheek, the eye of the sky god Horus that was torn out by his enemy Set and made whole again. One of the most common amulets of ancient Egypt, worn by the living and placed with the dead for protection, healing and wholeness. [src: wp]

## Myth & Origin

Horus, son of Osiris and Isis, fought his uncle Set, who had murdered Osiris, for the throne of Egypt. In the struggle Set tore out (or damaged) Horus's eye, sometimes said to be broken into pieces. Thoth, the god of wisdom, or in other tellings the goddess Hathor, restored it. Horus then offered his healed eye to his dead father Osiris, giving him new life in the underworld. [src: wp, bookdead]

Because the eye was wounded and restored, it became the sign of healing and of things made whole; because it was offered to Osiris, it became the model of every offering made to the gods; and because it belonged to the sky falcon whose eyes were the sun and moon, it was linked with the moon, which waxes and wanes and is made full again. [src: wp]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

Wedjat amulets of faience, stone and gold were made from the Old Kingdom to Roman times, in huge numbers. They were placed on mummies (often over the incision made for embalming), painted on coffins so the dead could see out, and worn by the living. Sailors painted eyes on the prows of boats. [src: wp]

### Medieval

With the end of Egyptian religion the amulet passed out of use, though eye-amulets against the evil eye continued all around the Mediterranean in other forms. [src: wp]

### Early modern

European travellers collected wedjat amulets as curiosities; occult writers made the "all-seeing eye" their own, though the Eye of Providence developed separately in Christian art. [src: wp]

### Modern

After the decipherment of hieroglyphs, Egyptologists studied the eye's meanings and its place in the arithmetic of grain measures. In the twentieth century it became a hugely popular tattoo, jewellery motif and emblem of modern Kemetic religion. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Faith:** a sacred sign of protection and healing for followers of Kemetism, the modern revival of Egyptian religion.
- **Jewellery and tattoos:** among the most popular ancient symbols, worn for protection and luck.
- **Design and popular culture:** logos, video games, films and music.
- **Folk belief:** in many Mediterranean countries eyes are still painted on boats for protection.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Ancient Egyptian

Wholeness restored after injury; protection of the body, alive and dead; the perfect offering to the gods; the moon renewed. [src: wp]

### As mathematics

A nineteenth- and twentieth-century theory held that the parts of the eye stood for the fractions 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, 1/32 and 1/64 used to measure grain. [src: wp]

### Modern esoteric

Writers have read it as the "third eye" or the eye of inner vision, and connected it, without historical basis, with the Eye of Providence on the American dollar bill. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Do the eye's parts stand for fractions?

- **Yes.** The "Horus-eye fractions" were long taught as a fact. [src: wp]
- **No, a later theory.** Scholars such as Jim Ritter have argued that the signs for the grain fractions only came to be linked with the eye in later interpretation, and that the link is not ancient. [src: wp]

### Eye of Horus or Eye of Ra?

- **Different eyes.** The Eye of Ra is the fierce sun-eye, a goddess sent out to punish; the Eye of Horus is the healed eye of the sky god, linked with the moon. [src: wp]
- **Often confused.** Egyptians themselves sometimes blended them, and modern use treats them as interchangeable; right and left eyes are sometimes assigned to sun and moon. [src: wp]

### Is ℞ from the Eye of Horus?

- **A popular claim** says the pharmacist's sign ℞ descends from the eye. [src: wp]
- **No evidence.** It comes from the Latin *recipe*, "take", in medieval prescriptions. [src: wp]

## Article

The Eye of Horus is a wound that heals. Its whole meaning lies in that sequence: an eye torn out in a fight for justice, put back together by wisdom, and given away as a gift to the dead father. Few symbols carry so complete a story of injury, repair and generosity.

The ancient Egyptians wore it by the million. A small blue-green faience eye on a cord at the throat, an eye painted on the side of a coffin, an eye placed over the embalmer's cut in a mummy's side: it guarded every opening and every vulnerability. It was protective because it had itself been damaged and made whole. What had been broken and restored could restore others.

It also had a cosmic meaning. Horus was the falcon of the sky, and his eyes were the sun and the moon. The injured eye was the moon, which shrinks, disappears and grows full again. Every month the sky retold the story of the wedjat.

Modern readers have given it other lives: as a set of fractions, as the third eye, as the eye on the dollar bill. Some of these links are scholarly theories, some pure invention. But the wish behind the amulet has not changed: to be watched over, and to be made whole.

For a storyteller, the Eye of Horus is a powerful gift: a symbol of the scar as strength, of loss that becomes the power to heal.
