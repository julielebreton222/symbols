## Overview

The legendary substance of the alchemists that could turn base metals like lead into gold and, many believed, heal diseases and lengthen life as an elixir. Making it was the Great Work (*magnum opus*). For some practitioners it was a chemical goal pursued in real laboratories; for others, and for many modern readers, also a symbol of spiritual perfection. [src: wp, principe]

## Myth & Origin

Alchemy grew in Greco-Roman Egypt in the first centuries CE, where metallurgy, Greek philosophy and Egyptian temple craft met. Zosimos of Panopolis (c. 300 CE) wrote of a transforming agent, a "tincture" or medicine that could perfect metals. [src: wp, principe]

Arabic alchemists, writing under the name of Jābir ibn Ḥayyān (8th–10th centuries), developed the theory that all metals are made of sulphur and mercury in different proportions, and spoke of an *al-iksīr*, the elixir, that could correct the balance. When their works were translated into Latin in the twelfth century, Europe received the idea of a single transforming stone, the *lapis philosophorum*. [src: wp]

Later legends attached it to famous names. Nicolas Flamel, a real fourteenth-century Paris scribe and benefactor, was said from the seventeenth century on to have made the stone and to be still alive. Many alchemical books describe the Work in colour stages: blackening (*nigredo*), whitening (*albedo*), yellowing (*citrinitas*) and reddening (*rubedo*), the red stone being the final perfection. [src: wp, curious]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

Greco-Egyptian alchemy (c. 1st–4th centuries CE): Zosimos, the *Chrysopoeia of Cleopatra* with its ouroboros, and the idea of a transforming tincture. [src: wp]

### Medieval

Arabic alchemy and its elixir (8th–10th centuries); Latin translations (12th century); the European alchemical tradition, with its allegorical images of kings, queens, dragons and lions. Rulers and popes alternately sponsored and condemned "multipliers" who promised gold; Pope John XXII issued a decree against fraudulent alchemists in 1317. [src: wp]

### Early modern

The golden age of alchemy: Paracelsus turned it toward medicine; emperors like Rudolf II gathered alchemists at Prague; Isaac Newton wrote over a million words on alchemy, and Robert Boyle sought the stone while founding modern chemistry. The Flamel legend took shape with a book published under his name in 1612. [src: wp, principe]

### Modern

Chemistry separated from alchemy in the eighteenth century, and the stone became a symbol. Carl Jung read it as the Self (*Psychology and Alchemy*, 1944). In 1919 Ernest Rutherford achieved the first artificial transmutation of an element, and particle physics later turned tiny amounts of other metals into gold, at a cost far above its value. J. K. Rowling's *Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone* (1997) made Flamel and the stone famous to millions of children. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Fiction:** *Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone* (in the US, *the Sorcerer's Stone*); *Fullmetal Alchemist*; Paulo Coelho's *The Alchemist*.
- **Language:** a "philosopher's stone" is a fabled solution to every problem.
- **Spirituality and psychology:** Jungian and esoteric writers use alchemy's stages as maps of inner transformation.
- **History of science:** a lively field studying alchemy as real laboratory practice that fed into chemistry.
[src: wp, principe]

## How it was seen

### As chemistry

For many alchemists the stone was a real material goal, sought with furnaces, glassware and careful recipes. Historians such as Lawrence Principe have reproduced alchemical experiments and shown how much real chemistry they contain. [src: principe]

### As medicine and long life

From the Arabic elixir to Paracelsus, the stone was also a universal medicine that could perfect the body as it perfected metals. [src: wp]

### As spiritual allegory

Many texts speak of the Work as a purification of the soul, with the stone as perfection or Christ. Some practitioners meant this literally, some symbolically, many both. [src: wp]

### Jungian

Jung read alchemical images as projections of the unconscious and the stone as the Self, the goal of individuation. Marie-Louise von Franz developed this reading. Compare the reference books' readings of alchemy and the stone. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Laboratory or soul?

- **Mostly real chemistry.** Held by historians like Lawrence Principe and William Newman: alchemists were practical experimenters, and the spiritual readings were exaggerated by nineteenth-century occultists and by Jung. [src: principe]
- **Spiritual and material at once.** Others stress that for medieval and early modern thinkers matter and spirit were not separate, so the Work could be both. [src: wp]
- **Mainly inner.** Esoteric and Jungian traditions read the texts primarily as maps of the soul. [src: wp]

### Was Newton a scientist who dabbled, or an alchemist who did science?

- **A serious alchemist.** His surviving manuscripts show long, careful alchemical work. John Maynard Keynes called him "the last of the magicians". [src: wp]
- **Part of a single quest.** Many historians now see his alchemy, theology and physics as one search for the hidden order of nature. [src: wp]

## Article

The philosopher's stone is the dream of a single substance that perfects everything it touches. Drop a little on molten lead and it becomes gold; take a little in wine and sickness leaves the body. Behind the dream was a theory that made sense for its time: if all metals are made of the same principles in different balances, then the right agent could correct an imperfect metal, as a doctor corrects a sick body. Gold was simply lead that had been healed.

For about fifteen hundred years, serious people worked toward it. They built furnaces, distilled, sublimed, recorded colours and smells, and wrote their results in a deliberately obscure language of dragons, green lions, royal marriages and death and rebirth. Some were frauds who promised kings gold; many were patient experimenters whose work became chemistry. Isaac Newton spent decades on it.

The colour sequence of the Work (black, white, yellow, red) is one of alchemy's great gifts to the imagination. First everything must be broken down and darkened, putrefied, "killed"; then washed white; then it ripens and reddens into the stone. It is easy to see why Jung and later readers found in it a map of the soul: no transformation without first going through the dark.

Today we can turn other metals into gold, in particle accelerators, a few atoms at a time and at enormous cost. The material dream has been fulfilled and made pointless at once. The symbolic dream has lasted better. In Harry Potter the stone gives endless life, and the wisest character chooses to destroy it.

For a writer, the stone is the archetype of the object everyone wants and no one should have: the cure-all, the infinite wealth, the escape from death. Its best stories are about what people become while they search for it.
