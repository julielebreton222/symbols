## Overview

The hammer of Thor, the Norse thunder god: forged by dwarves, it never misses, always returns to his hand, and can kill giants, raise slaughtered goats to life and bless a marriage. In the Viking Age people wore small hammers as amulets; today it is the best-known symbol of Heathenry, the modern revival of Norse religion. [src: wp, prose]

## Myth & Origin

Snorri Sturluson's *Prose Edda* (c. 1220) tells how it was made. Loki cut off the golden hair of Sif, Thor's wife, and to save his life promised to have the dwarves make her new hair. He then bet his head that the brothers Brokkr and Eitri (Sindri in some versions) could not make treasures as good as those of the sons of Ivaldi. As Eitri worked the forge, a fly (Loki in disguise) stung Brokkr to make him stop pumping the bellows; at the last moment it drew blood from his eyelid, he paused, and the hammer came out with a handle a little too short. Even so the gods judged it the best of all the treasures, for it would protect them against the giants. [src: prose]

Thor wields it with two other treasures: the iron gloves Járngreipr and the belt of strength Megingjörð. In the Eddic poem *Þrymskviða*, the giant Thrym steals the hammer and will return it only if Freyja marries him. Thor goes in her place, dressed as the bride; when the giants lay the hammer in the bride's lap to hallow the wedding, he seizes it and kills them all. [src: poetic, prose]

The hammer also blesses and restores. Thor hallows the funeral pyre of Baldr with it, and he kills his goats Tanngrisnir and Tanngnjóstr for supper, then raises them from their bones with Mjölnir the next morning. At Ragnarök, after Thor dies fighting the Midgard Serpent, his sons Móði and Magni inherit the hammer. [src: prose, poetic]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

Thunder gods with weapons appear across the Indo-European world: Indra's vajra, Zeus's thunderbolt, Perun's axe. Bronze Age rock carvings in Scandinavia show figures holding axes or hammers aloft, which some read as early thunder gods. [src: wp]

### Medieval

In the tenth century, small hammer pendants of silver, iron and amber were worn across Scandinavia and the Viking world, over a thousand of them now known. A soapstone mould from Trendgården in Denmark could cast hammers and Christian crosses side by side, a craftsman serving both faiths. Runestones such as the Glavendrup stone ask Thor to hallow the runes. The myths themselves were written down by Christian Icelanders in the thirteenth century: the *Poetic Edda* and Snorri's *Prose Edda*. [src: wp]

### Early modern

Icelandic magical staves and folk belief kept some of the old figures alive, while scholars from the seventeenth century began to publish and translate the Eddas. [src: wp]

### Modern

Nineteenth-century Romantic nationalism made Thor a national hero in art and poetry. In the twentieth century the hammer became the emblem of Heathenry (Ásatrú), reborn in Iceland in 1972, and of popular culture, from Marvel's comic-book Thor (from 1962) to heavy metal. In 2013 the US Department of Veterans Affairs added "Thor's Hammer" to the emblems of belief allowed on military headstones. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Faith:** worn as a pendant by many Heathens as a sign of their religion, much as Christians wear a cross; used to hallow rites such as weddings.
- **Military headstones:** an approved emblem of belief in the United States since 2013.
- **Popular culture:** the comic-book and film Thor, video games, and heavy metal imagery.
- **Misuse and dispute:** some white-supremacist groups have used the hammer; anti-hate organisations note that most people who wear it have nothing to do with them.
- **Jewellery and tattoos:** one of the most common Norse motifs in modern jewellery.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### In Viking Age belief

As far as archaeology shows, the hammer was a sign of protection and blessing, worn on the body and carved on stones, and possibly worn more in the tenth century as Christianity spread and people chose a symbol to set against the cross. [src: wp]

### In the medieval Icelandic texts

The Christian writers who preserved the myths treat Thor with humour and affection: his strength, his temper, his comic disguise as Freyja. The hammer is the guarantee that the gods' world holds against chaos. [src: prose, poetic]

### In comparative mythology

Scholars from Jacob Grimm onward have compared Thor with Indra, Zeus, Perun and the Baltic Perkūnas, and reconstructed a Proto-Indo-European storm god whose weapon strikes a serpent or monster to release water and fertility. [src: wp]

## Debates

### What does the name mean?

- **"Crusher", from the grinding of grain.** Linked to Old Norse words for grinding and meal. [src: wp]
- **"Lightning".** Compared with Russian *molniya* and Welsh *mellt*, both meaning lightning. [src: wp]
- **"White, shining".** Linked with Old Norse *mjöll*, fresh snow. Scholars have not settled the question. [src: wp]

### Were the pendants an answer to the cross?

- **Yes, a pagan counter-symbol.** Many archaeologists note that hammer pendants become common in the tenth century, just as crosses spread, and read them as a statement of identity. [src: wp]
- **Not only that.** Others stress that hammers protected and blessed (births, weddings, burials) for their own sake, and that the moulds for both show people moving between the faiths easily. [src: wp]

### Can a sacred sign be reclaimed from hate?

- **Heathen organisations** insist the hammer is a religious symbol and work to keep it from racist use. [src: wp]
- **Watch groups** list it among symbols sometimes used by extremists while warning against judging every wearer. [src: wp]

## Article

Thor's hammer is a weapon with a homely side. It smashes the skulls of giants, and it also raises a pair of goats from their bones so a poor farmer's family can eat again. It kills a wedding party, and it hallows a wedding. This doubleness (violence in the service of order, force that blesses) is what made the hammer the Norse symbol of protection.

Its making is a comedy of error. A fly stings, a smith pauses, the handle comes out short. The gods do not care: the hammer's power is in its striking face and in the fact that it always comes back. Norse myth often gives its greatest treasures a flaw, as if perfection were not quite the point. What matters is that the hammer works, and that it is on the side of the gods.

The *Þrymskviða* shows the hammer as the hinge of the cosmos. Without it the gods are helpless; to get it back, the god of thunder must put on a bridal veil and pretend to be the goddess of love. The poem is among the funniest in the Eddas, and also a little frightening: the order of the world depends on a single object, and that object can be stolen.

Archaeology gives the hammer a second life, on the necks of real people. In the tenth century men and women wore tiny hammers of silver and iron, and the mould from Trendgården cast hammers and crosses in the same stone. It is a vivid picture of an age of conversion, when the same smith served two gods and the same household might pray to both.

Today the hammer hangs again on the necks of believers, and its meaning is argued over. For Heathens it is a sacred sign of protection and kinship; for others, a comic-book prop; for a few, wrongly, a banner of hate. The old stories suggest an answer: the hammer belongs to the one who uses it to hold back chaos, not to the giants who steal it.
