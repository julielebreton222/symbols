## Overview

The six-pointed star made of two interlaced triangles, called in Hebrew *Magen David*, the "Shield of David". Today the most recognised symbol of Judaism and of the Jewish people, it stands at the centre of the flag of Israel. Its history as a specifically Jewish sign is younger and more surprising than many think. [src: wp, scholem]

## Myth & Origin

The hexagram is an ancient decorative shape found in many cultures. It appears in a synagogue frieze at Capernaum (from late antiquity) alongside other ornaments, but without special meaning. In medieval Jewish, Christian and Islamic magic, it was used as a protective sign and often called the Seal of Solomon. [src: wp, scholem]

The name "Shield of David" comes from Jewish mystical and magical texts of the Middle Ages, which spoke of a protective shield of God's name associated with King David. In the fourteenth century the Jewish community of Prague used the star on its flag, and from there it spread as an emblem of Jewish communities in Central Europe. [src: wp, scholem]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

A widespread geometric ornament, in Jewish as in non-Jewish settings, without a fixed meaning. The menorah, not the star, was the chief Jewish symbol of antiquity. [src: wp]

### Medieval

Hexagrams decorate Hebrew manuscripts such as the Leningrad Codex (1008) and appear on amulets as the Seal of Solomon. In Prague, from the fourteenth century, it became the emblem of the Jewish community. [src: wp, scholem]

### Early modern

Printers' marks, synagogues and communal seals spread it across Europe; by the seventeenth century it often marked Jewish quarters and institutions. [src: wp]

### Modern

In the nineteenth century, as emancipated Jews sought a symbol comparable to the Christian cross, the star became the general sign of Judaism. The Zionist movement adopted it at the First Zionist Congress (1897), and the flag of Israel placed it between two blue stripes in 1948. The Nazis forced Jews to wear a yellow star as a badge of persecution; after the Holocaust, the star became both a memorial and a sign of survival and pride. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Faith and identity:** on synagogues, prayer shawls, ritual objects, gravestones and jewellery; for many Jews it is a sign of belonging to the Jewish people and of faith in God's protection.
- **Israel:** at the centre of the national flag; the red Star of David is the emblem of Magen David Adom, Israel's emergency medical service.
- **Memory:** the yellow badge is remembered in Holocaust memorials and education.
- **Other uses:** the hexagram also appears in Christian and Islamic art, in Freemasonry and in esoteric diagrams, where it is not a Jewish symbol.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Medieval magic

A protective seal, the Seal of Solomon, in Jewish, Christian and Muslim amulets alike. [src: wp]

### Kabbalah

Some kabbalists read its two triangles as the union of upper and lower worlds, or linked its six points and centre with the seven lower sefirot. Such readings are later than the symbol's spread. [src: wp]

### Modern Jewish thought

For the scholar Gershom Scholem, the star's rise shows how a symbol can acquire meaning through history and suffering rather than through ancient origin. [src: scholem]

## Debates

### How old is it as a Jewish symbol?

- **Late, from the Middle Ages and after.** Held by Gershom Scholem ("The Curious History of the Six-Pointed Star", 1949): it became a general Jewish emblem only in early modern and modern times. [src: scholem]
- **Older roots in tradition.** Popular belief links it to King David's shield; scholars find no ancient evidence for this. [src: wp]

### Star of David and Seal of Solomon

- **Two names for one shape.** In medieval magic the hexagram (and sometimes the pentagram) was called the Seal of Solomon. [src: wp]
- **Different meanings today.** The Star of David is now a Jewish symbol; the Seal of Solomon belongs to the magical and Islamic traditions. [src: wp]

## Article

The Star of David is two triangles, one pointing up and one down, woven together. It is one of the simplest figures one can draw with a ruler, and for most of history it was just that: a pleasant pattern on a floor, a frieze or a page, found in churches and mosques as well as synagogues. How it became the sign of a people is one of the great stories of how symbols are made.

In the Middle Ages the shape belonged to magic. Jews, Christians and Muslims carved it on amulets as the Seal of Solomon, a sign of power over demons. Jewish mystical texts spoke of the "Shield of David", a protection woven of God's names. Then, in Prague, a Jewish community put the star on its banner, and from Central Europe it slowly became the badge of Jewish communities.

The nineteenth century completed the change. Jews entering modern society wanted a sign that could stand beside the cross and the crescent; the Zionist movement took it up; and the twentieth century gave it two opposite meanings at once. The Nazis turned it into a yellow badge of exclusion and death. Three years after the war, it was raised at the centre of the flag of Israel. Today it is both the memory of persecution and the sign of survival.

Gershom Scholem, who traced this history, saw it as an example of how a symbol earns its meaning: not from a legendary origin, but from what people have lived and suffered under it.

For a writer, the star shows a symbol whose power comes from history rather than myth, and which can carry grief and hope at the same time.
