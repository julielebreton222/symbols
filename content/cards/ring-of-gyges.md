## Overview

A gold ring that makes its wearer invisible when its bezel is turned inward: the centre of a thought experiment in Plato's *Republic*. A shepherd who finds it uses it to seduce a queen, kill a king and seize a throne. The question it asks is still alive: would anyone stay just if they could never be seen or caught? [src: republic, wp]

## Myth & Origin

In Book II of the *Republic*, Plato's brother Glaucon challenges Socrates with a story. Gyges, a shepherd serving the king of Lydia, was pasturing his flock when a storm and an earthquake opened a chasm in the ground. Climbing down, he found a hollow bronze horse with little doors, and inside it the corpse of a man larger than human, wearing nothing but a gold ring. Gyges took the ring. [src: republic]

Sitting later with the other shepherds, he happened to turn the ring's collet toward the palm of his hand and became invisible: the others spoke of him as if he had gone. Turning it outward, he reappeared. He contrived to be sent as a messenger to the palace, where he seduced the queen, with her help killed the king, and took the kingdom. [src: republic]

Glaucon's point: give two such rings to a just man and an unjust one, and they would soon behave alike, because people are just only out of fear of being seen. Socrates spends the rest of the *Republic* arguing the opposite: that justice is good for the soul itself, seen or unseen. [src: republic]

Herodotus tells an earlier, ring-less version: King Candaules of Lydia, proud of his wife's beauty, hid his bodyguard Gyges to watch her undress; the queen saw him and gave Gyges a choice: kill the king and marry her, or die. [src: herodotus]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

Gyges was a real king of Lydia in the seventh century BCE, founder of the Mermnad dynasty, mentioned in Assyrian records as "Gugu". Herodotus (5th century BCE) told his rise as a palace intrigue; Plato (4th century BCE) turned it into the ring story. Cicero retold it in *On Duties* (44 BCE) and concluded that a good man would act rightly even with such a ring. [src: wp, herodotus]

### Medieval

The *Republic* was little read in the Latin West, but magic rings of invisibility thrived in romance: Lunete's ring that hides Yvain in Chrétien de Troyes, Angelica's ring in Italian epic. [src: wp]

### Early modern

Renaissance humanists revived Plato and the ring; moralists used it in discussions of conscience. [src: wp]

### Modern

H. G. Wells's *The Invisible Man* (1897) and Tolkien's One Ring (1937, 1954) are often compared with Gyges. Philosophers use the "ring of Gyges" as a standard thought experiment about morality and observation, and recent writers apply it to online anonymity and surveillance. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Philosophy classrooms:** one of the most-discussed thought experiments in ethics.
- **Debates about technology:** anonymity on the internet, surveillance, and "what would you do if no one could see you?"
- **Fiction:** invisibility rings and cloaks in fantasy, from Tolkien to Harry Potter's invisibility cloak.
- **Popular psychology:** studies of how people behave when they believe they are unobserved are often framed with Gyges.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Glaucon's challenge

Justice is a compromise: people agree not to harm each other because they fear being harmed and punished. Remove the fear and justice disappears. [src: republic]

### Socrates' answer

Injustice corrupts the soul that commits it; the unjust man with the ring is a slave to his desires, the just man free. The whole *Republic* is an answer to the ring. [src: republic]

### Cicero

A wise man needs no ring: if he would not do a wrong deed in the open, he would not do it unseen. [src: wp]

### Modern readings

Psychologists and philosophers read the ring as a question about character and conscience; others read the corpse in the bronze horse as a hint that the ring's last owner did not end well. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Did Tolkien borrow from Plato?

- **Yes, at least as an echo.** Both rings grant invisibility and corrupt their owners, and Tolkien knew the classics. [src: wp]
- **No direct borrowing.** Tolkien never said he took the idea from Plato; his ring grows out of Norse and Germanic sources (Andvaranaut and the Nibelung treasure). [src: wp]

### Is Glaucon right?

- **Yes, for most people.** Studies of behaviour when unobserved, and the history of power, suggest that many people do behave worse when they think no one is watching. [src: wp]
- **No.** Plato's Socrates, Cicero and many ethicists argue that true virtue is not a performance for others. [src: republic]

## Article

The Ring of Gyges is a story with a philosopher's trap inside it. It sounds like a fairy tale: a storm, a crack in the earth, a bronze horse, a giant's corpse, a ring that makes you vanish. But Glaucon tells it in order to ask a hard question, and Plato builds an entire book to answer it.

The question is not "what would you do with such a ring?" (most of us can imagine) but "what does it show about you that you would do it?" If people are good only because they might be caught, then goodness is a kind of fear. Glaucon puts it baldly: hand the ring to the most just man in the city, and he will soon be stealing, seducing and killing like any tyrant.

The details of the story are full of quiet warnings. The ring comes from a tomb; its last owner is a corpse inside a hollow horse, larger than life and naked except for the ring. Gyges uses his invisibility for the oldest crimes there are, and the throne he wins is founded on murder. Herodotus, telling the earlier version, ends with a prophecy that Gyges' descendants will pay for it in the fifth generation, and they do, when Croesus loses his kingdom.

Today the ring is everywhere. Anyone online can be anonymous; cameras record nearly every street. The ancient question has split in two: what do we do when no one can see us, and what happens to us when everyone can?

For a storyteller, the ring is a perfect moral engine: a small object that removes consequences, and a character who discovers who they really are.
