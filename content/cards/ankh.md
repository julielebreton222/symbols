## Overview

The ancient Egyptian hieroglyph for "life": a cross whose upper arm is a loop. Gods hold it by the loop and offer it to the pharaoh's nose, giving him the breath of life. Egyptian Christians later took it as their cross, and today it is worn worldwide as a sign of life, of African heritage, and of the modern revival of Egyptian religion. [src: wp]

## Myth & Origin

In Egyptian writing the sign stood for the word *ꜥnḫ*, "life", and for other words with similar sounds, such as "mirror" and "sandal strap". In temple and tomb art gods carry it, pour streams of little ankhs over the king as purifying water, or hold it to his face so that he breathes in life. It appears in personal names, such as Tutankhamun, "living image of Amun". [src: wp]

No myth tells how the ankh was made: it is a sign rather than an object with a story. It often appears with two other signs, the *djed* pillar (stability) and the *was* sceptre (power), forming a wish for life, stability and dominion. [src: wp]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

The sign is found from the beginning of Egyptian writing (c. 3000 BCE) to the end of pagan Egypt. Amulets, mirror cases (one in Tutankhamun's tomb is shaped like an ankh), furniture and jewellery used it. The ankh travelled with Egyptian art into the Levant and Nubia. [src: wp]

### Medieval

Egyptian Christians (Copts) adopted the looped cross, the *crux ansata*, as a form of the Christian cross; it appears on Coptic textiles, carvings and manuscripts from late antiquity. The church historian Socrates Scholasticus reports that when the Serapeum of Alexandria was destroyed (391 CE), Christians and pagans alike read the cross-shaped signs found there as meaning "the life to come". [src: wp]

### Early modern

European travellers and scholars, fascinated by hieroglyphs they could not yet read, treated the ankh as a mystical "key of life". Champollion's decipherment (1822) confirmed that it meant "life". [src: wp]

### Modern

In the 1960s and 70s the ankh became a sign of the counterculture and of Black pride and African heritage, worn by musicians and activists. It is also popular in goth fashion and in modern Kemetic religion, which revives the worship of the Egyptian gods. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Faith:** a central sign for followers of Kemetism, the modern revival of ancient Egyptian religion, for whom it stands for the life given by the gods; still used in Coptic Christian art.
- **Heritage and identity:** worn as a sign of African and African-diaspora pride.
- **Fashion and jewellery:** pendants, earrings, tattoos; a staple of goth and of Egypt-inspired design.
- **Fiction:** familiar from comics, films and games set in or inspired by ancient Egypt.
- **The female symbol ♀:** a similar shape, but it comes from the astronomical sign of Venus, not from the ankh.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Ancient Egyptian

Life as a gift of the gods, passed to the king and through him to Egypt: something given, breathed, poured like water. [src: wp]

### Coptic Christian

The looped cross as the Cross of Christ, the tree of life and the promise of eternal life: a pagan sign read as a Christian prophecy. [src: wp]

### Esoteric and modern

From the Renaissance to the New Age, a "key" to hidden knowledge and to eternal life. Psychological readers see the union of a circle and a cross as a union of opposites. Such readings are modern, not Egyptian. [src: wp]

## Debates

### What did the sign first depict?

- **A sandal strap.** Proposed by the Egyptologist Alan Gardiner, because the same sign writes a word for sandal strap. [src: wp]
- **A knot or tie.** A ritual knot, perhaps related to the *tyet* sign of Isis. [src: wp]
- **Something else.** Suggestions have included a mirror, a girdle, and symbols of male and female union. Nobody knows for certain. [src: wp]

### Is ♀ an ankh?

- **No.** The Venus sign comes from Greek astronomical abbreviations, and the resemblance is a coincidence. [src: wp]
- **Popular belief says yes.** The two are often linked in modern writing. [src: wp]

## Article

The ankh is a word you can wear. Most symbols stand for something; the ankh simply *is* the word "life" in one of the world's oldest scripts, and Egyptians drew it the way we write a word on a birthday card. On temple walls the gods hold it out by its loop like a key, and the king breathes it in. Life, in Egyptian thinking, is not something you have but something you are given, again and again.

Its shape invites guesses. Is it a sandal strap, a knot, a mirror, a key? Egyptologists still disagree, and perhaps the Egyptians themselves did not think about it: they used the sign because it sounded like the word. But its form proved irresistible to later ages. To the Copts it was the Cross with a circle of eternity; to Renaissance mystics a key to forbidden wisdom; to the twentieth century a badge of rebellion, heritage and darkness all at once.

It is one of the few ancient sacred signs that slipped smoothly into Christianity without a fight. When Egyptians converted, they did not have to give up the sign of life; they simply gave it a new name.

For a storyteller the ankh is a reminder that a symbol can be a sound before it is an idea. A god holding out life on a loop of string is an image older than any myth told about it.
