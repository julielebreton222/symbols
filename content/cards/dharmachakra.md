## Overview

The wheel of the Dharma, the Buddha's teaching: a wheel, usually with eight spokes for the Noble Eightfold Path, which the Buddha "set turning" in his first sermon. One of the oldest and most widespread symbols of Buddhism, it also appears on the flag of India as the 24-spoked Ashoka Chakra. [src: wp, dhammapada]

## Myth & Origin

After his awakening under the Bodhi tree, the Buddha went to the Deer Park at Sarnath, near Varanasi, and taught his first five companions the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path. Buddhist tradition calls this first sermon the *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta*, the "discourse setting in motion the wheel of the Dharma". Once turned, the wheel of the teaching could not be stopped by any power in the world. [src: wp]

The image draws on older Indian ideas. A *chakravartin*, a "wheel-turning" universal monarch, was said to possess a divine wheel that rolled before his armies and conquered without violence. Buddhist texts say the Buddha could have become such a king but chose to turn the wheel of the Dharma instead. [src: wp]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

In the 3rd century BCE the emperor Ashoka, who embraced Buddhism, raised stone pillars across India; the Lion Capital at Sarnath carries wheels on its abacus. In the earliest Buddhist art (Sanchi, Bharhut), the Buddha is often not shown in human form at all: a wheel on a throne, flanked by deer, stands for his first sermon. [src: wp]

### Medieval

With Buddhism, the wheel spread across Asia: on temple roofs in Tibet (flanked by two deer), on stupas, in Chinese, Korean and Japanese art, and in the hands of buddhas making the "turning the wheel" teaching gesture (*dharmachakra mudra*). It is one of the eight auspicious symbols (Ashtamangala). [src: wp]

### Early modern

Monastic art and architecture across the Buddhist world kept the wheel as a central emblem. [src: wp]

### Modern

In 1947 independent India placed the Ashoka Chakra, with 24 spokes, at the centre of its flag, and the Lion Capital of Sarnath became the national emblem. The eight-spoked dharma wheel became the general emblem of Buddhism worldwide (Unicode ☸). [src: wp]

## Today

- **Buddhist practice:** on temples, flags, robes and books; the wheel with two deer crowns Tibetan monastery roofs. For Buddhists it means the Buddha's teaching and the path to freedom from suffering, turning in the world for the good of all beings.
- **India:** the Ashoka Chakra on the national flag, and the Ashoka Chakra award for peacetime gallantry.
- **Emblem of Buddhism:** used by Buddhist organisations worldwide and on military and hospital chaplaincy insignia.
- **Hinduism and Jainism:** wheels of dharma also appear in Hindu and Jain art; the Jain emblem includes a wheel in the palm of a hand.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Buddhist teaching

The eight spokes as the Eightfold Path (right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, concentration); the hub as moral discipline; the rim as concentration holding everything together. Different schools give different correspondences. [src: wp]

### Royal ideology

The wheel joined spiritual and political authority: Ashoka presented himself as ruling by dharma, a righteous king in the Buddha's image. [src: wp]

### Art history

The wheel is a classic case of "aniconism": the long period when the Buddha was shown by signs (a wheel, footprints, an empty throne, a tree) rather than a human figure. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Why was the Buddha first shown only by symbols?

- **A rule against images.** Older scholarship (Alfred Foucher and others) held that early Buddhists avoided depicting the Buddha. [src: wp]
- **Not absence but presence.** Susan Huntington argued that many such scenes show later pilgrims worshipping at holy sites, not a ban on images. The question is still debated. [src: wp]

### How many spokes?

- **Eight is common, but not fixed.** Wheels may have 8, 12, 24 or more spokes, and traditions explain the numbers differently (the Eightfold Path, the twelve links of dependent origination, and others). [src: wp]

## Article

The wheel of the Dharma is a symbol of motion. A wheel is meant to turn, and the Buddhist image is not of a wheel at rest but of one "set rolling": the teaching released into the world at Sarnath and travelling on by itself, from teacher to student, across centuries and countries. Once turned, nothing can turn it back.

Its power comes partly from a borrowed dream. Indian kings imagined a perfect ruler whose magic wheel rolled ahead of his armies and won the world without killing. The Buddhist stories turn this upside down: the prince who could have been that emperor chose instead to turn a different wheel, of insight rather than conquest. The image of world power became an image of a power over the mind.

For several centuries early Buddhist art showed the Buddha's first sermon not with a figure but with a wheel on a throne, flanked by two kneeling deer for the Deer Park. Whatever the reason, the effect is striking: the teaching itself is the presence. The same scene sits on Tibetan rooftops today.

Modern India put Ashoka's wheel at the centre of its flag, choosing a sign of righteousness and law over a sign of any single faith. The wheel has thus become both a sacred emblem and a national one.

For a storyteller, the dharma wheel is a beautiful image of an idea that, once spoken, moves by itself.
