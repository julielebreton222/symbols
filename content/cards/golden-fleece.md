## Overview

The fleece of a winged golden ram, hung in a sacred grove in Colchis at the eastern end of the Black Sea and guarded by a dragon that never slept. Jason and the Argonauts sailed to win it, and with the help of the sorceress Medea they did. Since antiquity it has stood for the impossible prize, for royal authority, and, in a famous order of knighthood, for chivalry itself. [src: wp, argonautica]

## Myth & Origin

King Athamas of Boeotia, persuaded by his second wife Ino, was about to sacrifice his children Phrixus and Helle. Their mother Nephele sent a golden ram, a gift of Hermes, which carried them away through the air. Helle fell into the strait that took her name, the Hellespont; Phrixus reached Colchis, sacrificed the ram to Zeus and gave its golden fleece to King Aeëtes, who hung it on an oak in the grove of Ares under the guard of a dragon. [src: wp, bulfinch]

Years later Pelias, who had seized the throne of Iolcus, sent his nephew Jason to bring back the fleece, expecting him to die. Jason gathered the greatest heroes of Greece (Heracles, Orpheus, the twins Castor and Pollux and others) on the ship *Argo*. In Colchis, Aeëtes set impossible tasks: yoke fire-breathing bronze bulls, sow dragon's teeth and kill the warriors that sprang from them. Medea, the king's daughter, who had fallen in love with Jason, gave him a protective ointment and, in the end, put the dragon to sleep with her drugs and songs. Jason took the fleece and fled with her. [src: argonautica, pindar]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

Homer and Hesiod already knew the *Argo*. Pindar's fourth *Pythian Ode* (462 BCE) tells the voyage, Euripides' *Medea* (431 BCE) its bitter aftermath, and Apollonius of Rhodes' *Argonautica* (3rd century BCE) the full epic. The Roman Valerius Flaccus retold it in Latin. [src: wp]

### Medieval

Christian writers read the fleece allegorically, even as an image of the Virgin (Gideon's fleece) or of Christ. The *Roman de Troie* and other romances retold Jason's quest as a knightly adventure. [src: wp]

### Early modern

In 1430 Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, founded the Order of the Golden Fleece, whose collar ends in a golden ram; it passed to the Habsburgs and survives in Spanish and Austrian branches. [src: wp]

### Modern

The fleece became a byword for any great prize; the films *Jason and the Argonauts* (1963) with Ray Harryhausen's skeleton warriors fixed it in popular imagination. Archaeologists and geologists have explored the old idea that it remembers gold-panning with sheepskins in Georgia. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Language:** a "golden fleece" is a coveted prize; in American politics the "Golden Fleece Award" mocked wasteful spending.
- **Chivalry:** the Order of the Golden Fleece still exists; King Felipe VI of Spain is its sovereign.
- **Georgia:** the country embraces the myth as part of its ancient history.
- **Fiction, film and games:** a favourite quest object.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Greek

A royal talisman: the kingdom that holds it is secure; winning it proves a hero's right to rule. [src: pindar]

### Allegory

Christian and Renaissance writers turned it into an image of virtue, wisdom, or the reward of the soul. [src: wp]

### Alchemy

Some alchemists read the fleece as a coded recipe for gold, or the Philosopher's Stone. [src: wp]

### Rationalist

Ancient and modern writers explained it as a memory of gold-washing with fleeces. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Was there a real "golden fleece"?

- **Gold-panning with sheepskins.** The geographer Strabo described people of the Caucasus catching gold from mountain streams with fleeces; some scholars and geologists see the origin of the myth here. [src: wp]
- **Pure myth.** Others think the story needs no real fleece: a golden ram is a natural image of kingship and the sun. [src: wp]

### Hero or villain?

- **Jason the hero.** In Pindar and Apollonius, Jason is the chosen hero who wins the prize. [src: pindar]
- **Medea's story.** Euripides and many modern readers see the quest through Medea, the foreign woman who gives everything and is betrayed. [src: wp]

## Article

The Golden Fleece is a prize that costs more than it is worth. It begins with a rescue: a mother sends a golden ram to save her children from sacrifice, and one of them falls into the sea. It ends, after Jason brings it home, with murder, exile and Medea killing her own children. Between the two stands one of the great adventure stories of the ancient world.

Its power lies partly in the ship. The *Argo* carries the heroes of a whole generation to the edge of the known world, through clashing rocks and past strange peoples. The voyage is the first great "team" story: a fellowship of heroes, each with a gift, sailing for a shining object at the end of the map.

But the fleece is won by Medea, not by muscle. She is the one who knows the drugs and the spells; she yokes the story to her desire and pays for it. Euripides saw this clearly, and modern writers have retold the myth from her side: the foreign princess who betrays her father for a stranger and is abandoned in a land that despises her.

The fleece later became a Burgundian order of knighthood and an expression for any great prize. Geologists still pan for gold with sheepskins in the mountains of Georgia, and like to think they are doing what the people of Colchis did.

For a storyteller, the fleece is the model of the quest object whose winning is only the middle of the story.
