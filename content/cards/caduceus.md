## Overview

The winged staff of Hermes (Roman Mercury), with two snakes twined around it: originally the wand of a herald, which made its bearer untouchable on a diplomatic errand. It stands for messages, commerce, eloquence and safe passage, including the passage of souls to the underworld. In the twentieth century it was widely adopted, by mistake, as a symbol of medicine. [src: wp, hymn]

## Myth & Origin

The Greek word *kērykeion* means "herald's staff". Heralds in ancient Greece carried such staffs as signs of their office, and a herald was protected even among enemies. Hermes, the messenger of the gods, carries the most splendid one. [src: wp]

The *Homeric Hymn to Hermes* tells how the infant Hermes stole Apollo's cattle, invented the lyre from a tortoise shell, and soothed Apollo's anger by giving it to him. In exchange Apollo gave Hermes a golden staff of wealth and good fortune. [src: hymn]

Later writers explained the snakes. The Roman author Hyginus says Mercury once came upon two snakes fighting, laid his staff between them, and they made peace and wound around it; so the caduceus became a sign of peace. Mercury also uses it to put mortals to sleep and wake them, and to lead the souls of the dead. [src: hyginus, wp]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

Twined serpents are older than Greece: a Mesopotamian libation vase of Gudea of Lagash (c. 2100 BCE) shows two snakes coiled around a rod, the emblem of the god Ningishzida. In Greek vase painting from the sixth century BCE Hermes carries the kerykeion; Roman coins show the caduceus as a sign of peace and prosperity. [src: wp]

### Medieval

Mercury survived as a planet and as a figure of eloquence; in alchemy, Mercury and his caduceus came to stand for quicksilver and the union of opposites. The astronomical symbol of the planet Mercury (☿) is thought to derive from the caduceus. [src: wp]

### Early modern

Renaissance emblem books used the caduceus for eloquence, peace and commerce. The Basel printer Johann Froben made a caduceus his printer's device, and printers of medical books often followed: one route by which the staff came to be associated with medicine. [src: wp]

### Modern

In 1902 the US Army Medical Corps adopted the caduceus as its insignia, and many American medical organisations and companies followed. Critics have protested ever since that the true emblem of medicine is the one-snake [[rod-of-asclepius|Rod of Asclepius]]. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Medicine (mostly in the United States):** on ambulances, hospital signs, pharmacy logos and the uniforms of the US Army Medical Department, though professional bodies like the American Medical Association and the World Health Organization use the Rod of Asclepius.
- **Commerce and messengers:** emblems of trade, customs services and postal and courier companies.
- **Astronomy and astrology:** the planet Mercury's symbol ☿.
- **Esotericism:** in modern occult and New Age writing the two snakes are compared with energies rising along the spine.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Greek and Roman: the herald's peace

A sign of inviolable messengers, of truce and negotiation, and of Hermes's gifts: luck, profit, cleverness, travel. [src: wp, hymn]

### Alchemy: the marriage of opposites

Alchemists read the two snakes as the opposed principles (sulphur and mercury, fixed and volatile, male and female) bound in balance on one axis. [src: wp]

### Renaissance emblem books

Peace, eloquence and concord: the caduceus between two hands meant a treaty or friendship. [src: wp]

### Modern medicine and its critics

Its medical use rests on confusion with the Rod of Asclepius and on Hermes's role as god of commerce, which critics find unfortunate for a symbol of healing. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Is the caduceus a symbol of medicine?

- **No, a mistake.** Hermes was the god of heralds, merchants, travellers and thieves, and the guide of the dead, not of healing. The true symbol is the Rod of Asclepius. Held by many historians of medicine and by professional bodies such as the WHO and the AMA. [src: wp]
- **It has become one.** Usage has made the caduceus a widely understood sign of medical care, especially in the United States, whatever its origin. [src: wp]
- **A partial link.** Some note that in late antiquity and the Renaissance Hermes was linked to alchemy and secret knowledge (as Hermes Trismegistus), and printers of medical books used his staff, so the confusion has a history of its own. [src: wp]

### Where do the twin snakes come from?

- **A Near Eastern inheritance.** The twined snakes of Ningishzida suggest a much older Mesopotamian image. [src: wp]
- **A Greek invention.** Others see the herald's staff as originally a forked or knotted stick, the snakes being a later reading of its shape. [src: wp]

## Article

The caduceus is a symbol that has drifted. It began as a herald's stick, the badge of a man you must not harm because he carried words between enemies. Hermes, the quickest of the gods, made it beautiful: gold, winged, wound with snakes. In his hands it opened doors between worlds. He used it to guide souls down to Hades and to bring dreams; it could put any eye to sleep.

The snakes are its mystery. Hyginus's story (two serpents quarrelling, separated by the god's staff and bound together in peace) turns them into a picture of reconciliation. Alchemists saw opposites held in balance. Much older, a vase made for a Sumerian king shows the same twin coil around a rod, the sign of a god of the underworld and vegetation.

Then, in the age of print, the symbol drifted further. A Basel printer took the caduceus as his mark; medical publishers followed; in 1902 the US Army's doctors put it on their collars. Since then generations of physicians have been reminded, in letters to journals, that the god of medicine was Asclepius, whose staff has only one snake and no wings, and that Hermes was also the patron of merchants and thieves.

The error is revealing. A symbol does not mean only what its origin says; it means what people use it for. Today the caduceus on an ambulance means help is coming, and in that sense it has returned to its first job: a sign that the bearer must be let through.

For a writer, the caduceus offers a beautiful hinge: an object of passage. Whoever holds it can cross borders, wake the sleeping, lead the dead, and make enemies talk.
