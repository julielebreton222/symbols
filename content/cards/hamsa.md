## Overview

A palm-shaped amulet, usually with the thumb and little finger turned outward symmetrically, often with an eye in the centre. Popular across North Africa and the Middle East among Jews, Muslims and Christians alike, it protects against the evil eye and brings blessing. Its names come from the word for "five": Arabic *khamsa*, Hebrew *hamesh*. [src: wp]

## Myth & Origin

There is no single founding myth. Muslim tradition, especially in North Africa, calls it the Hand of Fatima, after the Prophet Muhammad's daughter; Jewish tradition sometimes calls it the Hand of Miriam, after the sister of Moses and Aaron; Christians in the region have called it the Hand of Mary. Each community has read its own holy figure into the same ancient gesture. [src: wp]

Its power is to turn back the evil eye: the envious gaze believed to cause illness, misfortune and the death of children and livestock. Many hamsas include an eye, which "looks back" at the evil eye; many are blue, a colour thought to repel it. [src: wp]

Hand amulets are very old in the region. Some scholars link the hamsa to the raised hand of the Phoenician and Carthaginian goddess Tanit, or to hand signs on Mesopotamian amulets; the connection is possible but not proven. [src: wp]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

Open hands appear on amulets, stelae and in rock art across the ancient Mediterranean and Near East; Carthaginian stelae often show a raised hand. [src: wp]

### Medieval

In Muslim Spain and North Africa, hand amulets were common; the Gate of Justice of the Alhambra in Granada (1348) is carved with a hand above its arch. Jewish communities of Morocco, Tunisia and Iraq used hand amulets on doors, cradles and wedding objects. [src: wp]

### Early modern

Silver and brass hamsas, often engraved with blessings or verses, were made by Jewish and Muslim silversmiths across the Maghreb and the Ottoman world. [src: wp]

### Modern

After the emigration of North African Jews to Israel and France, the hamsa became one of the best-known symbols of Israeli and Mizrahi Jewish culture; it is also everywhere in Moroccan, Algerian and Tunisian homes and in Middle Eastern tourism, and has become a global fashion and jewellery motif. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Protection at home:** hung over doors, in cars, over babies' cribs, and at weddings across North Africa, the Middle East and Israel.
- **What it means to those who use it:** for many Jewish and Muslim families it is a sign of God's protection and blessing, and a link to the faith and home of their grandparents; for others it is simply a sign of good luck.
- **Jewellery and fashion:** pendants, earrings and tattoos worldwide.
- **Interfaith symbol:** sometimes used as a sign of shared heritage between Jews and Muslims.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Folk religion

The hand stops the evil eye as a raised palm stops someone in their tracks. The number five links it to Muslim and Jewish sacred fives (the Five Pillars, the five books of the Torah). [src: wp]

### Religious authorities

Some rabbis and some Muslim scholars have frowned on amulets as superstition, or in Islam as a form of *shirk* (associating something with God's power); others tolerate them, especially when inscribed with prayers or verses. [src: wp]

### Modern scholarship

A shared Mediterranean folk practice that crosses religious lines, later given different religious names. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Is the hamsa religious or folk?

- **Folk custom.** Many scholars and religious authorities treat it as a popular amulet, not a religious symbol. [src: wp]
- **Part of lived religion.** For many families it is inseparable from their faith and is inscribed with blessings, prayers or divine names. [src: wp]

### "Hand of Fatima": an apt name?

- **A traditional name.** Used in parts of the Muslim world, especially the Maghreb. [src: wp]
- **A misleading one.** Some Muslims object to associating Fatima with an amulet; some scholars note the name is more common in Western books than in everyday use. [src: wp]

### Is it descended from Tanit?

- **Possibly.** The raised hands of Carthaginian art are in the same region. [src: wp]
- **Not shown.** There is no continuous evidence linking them. [src: wp]

## Article

The hamsa is the gesture "stop!" turned into a charm. A raised open hand, five fingers spread or pressed together, faces outward toward whatever danger is coming. In much of North Africa and the Middle East, the danger it stops is the evil eye: the envious, admiring, harmful gaze that can make a baby sick or a marriage fail. The hand stands between you and that look; often an eye in its palm stares back.

It is one of the few symbols shared so fully by Jews and Muslims. A Moroccan Jewish grandmother and her Muslim neighbour might have hung nearly identical silver hands over their doors, the one calling it the hand of Miriam, the other the hand of Fatima. Each faith gave a holy woman's name to an older shared habit of protection. It is a small sign of how much the peoples of the region have in common.

Religious authorities have not always approved. Some rabbis and some Muslim scholars have warned that trusting an amulet can come close to trusting something other than God. But for most of the families who hang it, the hamsa is not a rival to faith but a part of it: a blessing for the house, often inscribed with a prayer.

Today the hamsa hangs in Paris apartments, Tel Aviv taxis, Marrakech markets and on Instagram. It has become fashion, but the impulse it expresses is old and universal: to raise a hand against harm, and to keep the people we love safe from the eyes of others.

For a storyteller the hamsa offers a rich, intimate object: a hand on a door, passed from mother to daughter, carrying a family's fears and blessings.
