## Overview

The most sacred sound and syllable in Hinduism, also central in Buddhism, Jainism and (as Ik Onkar) Sikhism. It is chanted at the beginning and end of prayers, readings and meditation, and written with the sign ॐ. For Hindus it is the sound of Brahman, the ultimate reality: the whole universe in one syllable. [src: wp, gita]

## Myth & Origin

Om (or Aum) is first prominent in the Upanishads, the philosophical texts of the late Vedic period. The *Chandogya Upanishad* opens by praising Om as the essence of the Vedic chant. The short *Mandukya Upanishad* explains its three sounds, A, U and M, as the three states of consciousness (waking, dreaming and dreamless sleep) and the silence after it as the fourth state, *turiya*, pure consciousness. [src: wp]

In the *Bhagavad Gita*, Krishna says that whoever leaves the body uttering Om, the one syllable that is Brahman, and remembering him, reaches the highest goal; and that Om, Tat, Sat is the threefold name of Brahman. [src: gita]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

The syllable was used in Vedic chanting before the Upanishads gave it philosophical meaning (c. 800–300 BCE). Buddhism and Jainism, which grew up in the same world, adopted it in their own ways. [src: wp]

### Medieval

The written sign ॐ spread through manuscripts and temple inscriptions across South and Southeast Asia. In Buddhist mantras such as *Om mani padme hum* (Tibet) it begins countless prayers. In Jainism Om is explained as joining the first sounds of the five supreme beings (Arihant, Ashariri/Siddha, Acharya, Upadhyaya and Muni). Guru Nanak's *Ik Onkar*, "One Creator", opens the Sikh scripture. [src: wp]

### Early modern

Om remained at the centre of Hindu devotion, yoga and Vedanta philosophy, and of the Sanskrit learning that Europeans began to study in the eighteenth century. [src: wp]

### Modern

From the late nineteenth century, teachers such as Swami Vivekananda brought Hindu philosophy and yoga to the West, and Om with them. Since the 1960s it has become a worldwide sign of yoga and meditation; it is encoded in Unicode as ॐ (U+0950). [src: wp]

## Today

- **Worship:** chanted at the start and end of prayers, mantras, recitations of scripture and rituals; written on doors, shrines and wedding invitations.
- **What it means to believers:** for Hindus, Om is the primordial sound and the name of God, containing all of creation; chanting it is a way to quiet the mind and turn toward the divine. Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs each hold it (or Ik Onkar) sacred in their own ways.
- **Yoga and meditation worldwide:** chanted at the opening and close of classes.
- **Commerce and controversy:** Om on clothing, mats, bags, shoes and tattoos; some Hindus welcome its spread, others object when it appears on shoes, swimwear or alcohol.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Upanishadic philosophy

The whole of reality in a single sound: A, U, M for the three states of consciousness, and the silence that follows for the absolute. [src: wp]

### Devotional (bhakti)

The name of God, especially of Shiva, Vishnu or Ganesha, whose form is sometimes seen in the shape of the written sign. [src: wp]

### Yoga

The sound that steadies the breath and the mind; Patanjali's *Yoga Sutras* call the syllable the expression of Ishvara, the Lord. [src: wp]

### Western reception

For many in the West Om means calm, spirituality and yoga, often detached from its Hindu theology. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Sacred sign or global brand?

- **Sharing is welcome.** Many Hindu teachers see the worldwide chanting of Om as a gift of Indian spirituality to the world. [src: wp]
- **Respect is needed.** Others object to Om printed on shoes, bathmats, swimwear or beer labels, and to its use without understanding; some campaigns have persuaded companies to withdraw products. [src: wp]

### What does the shape mean?

- **Traditional readings.** The curves of ॐ are often explained as the three states of consciousness, the dot as turiya and the crescent as maya. [src: wp]
- **A script development.** Historians of writing see the sign as a ligature of Devanagari letters that developed over time. [src: wp]

## Article

Om is a symbol you can hear. Most sacred signs are images; this one is first a sound, a hum that begins open in the throat and closes on the lips, and then a silence. Hindus have chanted it for some three thousand years, at the start of a prayer, before reading scripture, at the moment of death. To say it is, in a sense, to say everything.

The Upanishads made it a philosophy. The *Mandukya* takes the three sounds A, U, M and gives each a state of consciousness: waking, dreaming, deep sleep. Then it points to the silence after the M, the fourth state, which is none of these and the ground of all of them. It is one of the most elegant ideas in the history of religion: a single syllable as a map of the mind, with its deepest truth in the quiet after the sound.

The same syllable opens Buddhist mantras in Tibet, is read by Jains as the names of their five holy beings, and in Sikh scripture becomes *Ik Onkar*, "One Creator". Few signs have been so widely shared by neighbouring faiths.

In the modern world Om has travelled further still: into yoga studios, onto mats and T-shirts and tattoos. For many Hindus this is a source of pride; for some, of discomfort when the sacred sound becomes a logo. The question is the old one for every sacred sign that travels: how to share it without emptying it.

For a writer, Om offers something rare: a symbol made of breath, whose meaning lies as much in silence as in sound.
