## Overview

A flower that rises clean and bright from muddy water: in Hinduism and Buddhism the great sign of purity, divine birth and awakening, the seat of gods and buddhas; in ancient Egypt (where the "lotus" was a blue water lily) a sign of the sun and of rebirth, because it opens at dawn and closes at night. [src: wp, wp:Nymphaea caerulea]

## Myth & Origin

In Hindu myth, when Vishnu lies asleep on the cosmic ocean, a lotus grows from his navel, and on it sits Brahma, who creates the world. Lakshmi, goddess of fortune and beauty, stands or sits on a lotus and holds lotuses in her hands; Saraswati sits on a white lotus. The lotus throne (*padmasana*) became the seat of divinity in Indian art. [src: wp]

In Buddhist tradition the newborn Buddha took seven steps and lotuses sprang up where he walked. The flower rooted in mud, growing through water and opening clean in the air, became the image of a mind rising through the world's confusion to awakening. The *Lotus Sutra*, one of the most influential Mahayana scriptures, takes its name from it. [src: wp]

In Egypt the god Nefertem was born from a blue lotus, and the creator sun rose from a lotus on the primeval waters. The Book of the Dead has a spell for being transformed into a lotus. [src: bookdead, wp:Nymphaea caerulea]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

Egyptian art from the Old Kingdom on is full of blue and white water lilies: in offerings, in the hands of guests at banquets, as capitals of columns. In India the lotus appears in the Vedas and becomes a dominant motif in Buddhist art from the time of Ashoka (3rd century BCE), long before the Buddha himself was shown in human form. The Persians and Assyrians used rosettes and lotus motifs in palaces. [src: wp]

### Medieval

Across Asia the lotus throne carried buddhas, bodhisattvas and Hindu gods in sculpture and painting; temple plans and mandalas used its petals as patterns of the cosmos. In China it became a Confucian emblem of the upright gentleman, praised in Zhou Dunyi's essay *On Loving the Lotus* (11th century). [src: wp]

### Early modern

In Mughal and Rajput painting, in Tibetan thangkas and in East Asian ceramics the lotus remained the most beloved sacred flower. [src: wp]

### Modern

The lotus is the national flower of India and of Vietnam and the symbol of India's Bharatiya Janata Party. The Bahá'í House of Worship in Delhi, the Lotus Temple (1986), is built as a flower of twenty-seven petals. Botanists named the "lotus effect" (the self-cleaning leaves on which water beads and rolls away) after it, and grew a lotus from a seed about 1,300 years old found in China. [src: wp]

## Today

- **Hindu worship:** lotuses are offered in puja; gods are shown on lotus thrones; the "lotus feet" of a deity or guru are honoured. To believers it means divine beauty, purity and the unfolding of the soul.
- **Buddhist practice:** the lotus position (*padmasana*) in meditation; lotus lanterns at the Buddha's birthday; the lotus as the image of awakening possible to everyone.
- **Nations and parties:** national flower of India and Vietnam; logo of India's ruling BJP.
- **Architecture and design:** the Lotus Temple in Delhi; countless logos, tattoos and yoga-studio signs.
- **Science:** the "lotus effect" of self-cleaning surfaces.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Hindu: the seat of the divine

From the lotus of Vishnu's navel comes creation; on lotuses stand the gods of wisdom and wealth. The *Bhagavad Gita* (5.10) compares the person who acts without attachment to a lotus leaf that water cannot wet. [src: gita, wp]

### Buddhist: purity out of mud

The flower that grows from mud but is not soiled by it shows that awakening happens in the world, not apart from it. The *Dhammapada* compares the disciple of the Buddha to a fragrant flower growing on a heap of rubbish by the roadside. [src: dhammapada]

### Egyptian: the sun reborn

Because the water lily closes at night and opens at dawn, the Egyptians linked it to the sun's daily rebirth and to the hope of rising again after death. [src: wp:Nymphaea caerulea]

### Psychological readings

Jungian writers have read the many-petalled lotus as an image of the Self unfolding, and the lotus centres in yogic chakra diagrams as stages of awakening. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Which flower is the "lotus"?

- **Two different plants.** The Indian sacred lotus (*Nelumbo nucifera*) and the Egyptian blue and white "lotuses" (*Nymphaea* water lilies) are not related, though both are called lotus in older books. [src: wp, wp:Nymphaea caerulea]
- **One symbol, two plants.** In symbolism they share so much (water, the sun, rebirth) that many writers treat them together. [src: wp]

### What does *Om mani padme hum* mean?

- **"The jewel in the lotus".** The popular Western reading of the Tibetan mantra. [src: wp:Om mani padme hum]
- **An invocation of a deity.** Many scholars read *maṇipadme* as a vocative, calling on a "jewel-lotus" bodhisattva (Avalokiteshvara), rather than a sentence about a jewel inside a lotus. [src: wp:Om mani padme hum]

### The lotus of the Lotus-eaters

- **A different plant again.** The "lotus" that makes Odysseus's men forget home in the *Odyssey* was probably a fruit (perhaps the jujube), not a water flower. [src: wp:Lotophagi]

## Article

The lotus is the flower of beginnings. In India it opens from the navel of a sleeping god and holds the creator; in Egypt it rises from the first waters carrying the newborn sun. In both places people watched the same thing happen every day: a bud lifting from dark water and opening in the light. Few images are so natural and so complete.

The Buddhists saw something more. A lotus grows from mud. Its roots are in the muck at the bottom of the pond, its stem passes through cloudy water, and yet the flower is spotless; water rolls off its leaves without wetting them. It became the perfect image of a life that is in the world without being stained by it, and of an awakening that does not require escape. The mud is not the enemy of the lotus; it is its soil.

That is why the lotus can sit beneath the most transcendent figures in Asian art (buddhas, goddesses, saints) and still remain a garden plant. Children float lotus lanterns on the Buddha's birthday; gardeners grow lotuses in pots; scientists study their self-cleaning leaves; an ancient seed sleeps for over a thousand years and still sprouts.

The Egyptian lotus is a different flower, a water lily, but its story rhymes: it closes at dusk and opens at dawn, so it was the flower of the sun, of daily rebirth and of the hope of rising after death. In the Book of the Dead the soul asks to be transformed into one.

For a writer, the lotus is one of the great images of transformation without denial: becoming beautiful not by leaving the mud but by growing through it.
