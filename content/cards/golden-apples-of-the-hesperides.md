## Overview

Golden fruit from a tree that Earth gave to Hera as a wedding gift, growing in a garden at the western edge of the world and guarded by the nymphs of evening, the Hesperides, and a hundred-headed serpent. Heracles fetched them in one of his Labours; golden apples also won the race against Atalanta and, thrown by the goddess of strife, began the Trojan War. [src: wp, hesiod]

## Myth & Origin

When Zeus married Hera, Gaia (Earth) gave her a tree bearing golden apples. Hera planted it in a garden far to the west, near where Atlas holds up the sky, and set the Hesperides (daughters of the Evening) and the serpent Ladon to guard it. [src: wp, bulfinch]

For one of his twelve Labours, Heracles had to bring back the apples. In the best-known version he persuaded Atlas to fetch them while he held the sky in Atlas's place; Atlas returned and wanted to leave Heracles holding it forever, but Heracles tricked him into taking it back "just for a moment" and walked off with the apples. In other versions Heracles kills the serpent himself. [src: bulfinch, wp]

Golden apples appear in two more famous stories. Hippomenes won the swift huntress Atalanta, who would only marry a man who could outrun her, by dropping three golden apples from Aphrodite during the race; she stopped to pick them up and lost. And Eris, goddess of strife, uninvited to a wedding, threw among the guests a golden apple inscribed "For the fairest"; Paris's judgement between Hera, Athena and Aphrodite led to the Trojan War. [src: ovid, wp]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

Hesiod names the Hesperides; vase painters loved the scene of the garden with its tree and coiled serpent; the Labour appears on the metopes of the Temple of Zeus at Olympia (5th century BCE). Ancient writers placed the garden in Libya, near the Atlas mountains, or beyond the Pillars of Heracles. [src: wp]

### Medieval

Medieval writers read Heracles's Labours as allegories of virtue; golden apples entered romance and heraldry. [src: wp]

### Early modern

Renaissance gardens and paintings celebrated the Hesperides; the orange and lemon trees of princely gardens were called *hesperidia*, and the word survives in botany for citrus fruit. [src: wp]

### Modern

Victorian painters such as Frederic Leighton (*The Garden of the Hesperides*, 1892) gave the myth new life; the golden apple of discord gave its name to modern stories and even to a parody religion (Discordianism). [src: wp]

## Today

- **Botany:** a *hesperidium* is the botanical name for citrus fruits like oranges and lemons.
- **Language:** an "apple of discord" is a cause of quarrel.
- **Art and literature:** a favourite subject for painters, poets and fantasy writers.
- **Games:** golden apples of immortality or healing are common in video games.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Greek

Divine fruit at the end of the world: immortality, marriage and the gods' property, which a hero can take only by cunning or great labour. [src: hesiod]

### Allegorical

Medieval and Renaissance moralists read the apples as virtues won by Heracles, and Atalanta's apples as the temptations of wealth. [src: wp]

### Modern psychological

Golden fruit at the edge of the world as an image of the precious and unreachable, the reward of the hero's journey. [src: wp]

## Debates

### Were they apples?

- **Golden apples.** The Greek word *mēla* could mean apples. [src: wp]
- **Other fruit.** *Mēla* was used for many round fruits; some suggest quinces, or later oranges, which reached the Mediterranean much later and were then named after the myth. [src: wp]

### Where was the garden?

- **North Africa.** Many ancient writers placed it in Libya, near Mount Atlas; the ancient city of Euesperides (modern Benghazi) took its name from it. [src: wp]
- **Beyond the known world.** Others placed it at the ocean's edge in the far west. [src: wp]

## Article

Golden apples are the treasure at the far end of the world. To get to them you must travel past where the sun sets, to the garden of the daughters of Evening, where the Titan Atlas holds up the sky and a serpent coils around the tree. They are a wedding gift from Earth to the queen of the gods: the most precious thing the world can produce, kept for the gods alone.

Heracles gets them not by strength but by a trick, and a trick is played on him first. Atlas offers to fetch the apples if Heracles will hold the sky, and then wants to leave him there forever. The greatest hero in Greece escapes only by asking Atlas to hold the sky "for a moment" while he adjusts his cloak. Even the strongest need cunning.

Golden apples are also the most dangerous gifts in Greek myth. Three of them win a race and a bride, because the fastest runner in Greece cannot resist stopping. One of them, thrown into a wedding by the goddess of strife, starts the Trojan War. Gold is beautiful, and it distracts.

The myth has left traces in odd places. Botanists call citrus fruits *hesperidia*. People still speak of an "apple of discord". Painters keep returning to the quiet garden at sunset where three women sit under a golden tree.

For a storyteller, golden apples are the classic temptation: precious, shining, and always thrown at exactly the wrong moment.
