## Overview

A Sanskrit word meaning both "thunderbolt" and "diamond": the weapon of the Vedic god Indra, and in Buddhism a ritual sceptre standing for the indestructible clarity of awakening. Held with a bell in Tibetan ritual, it is one of the central objects of Vajrayana, the "Diamond Vehicle". [src: wp, rigveda]

## Myth & Origin

In the Rigveda, the oldest Indian scripture, Indra drinks soma and strikes down the serpent-demon Vritra with his vajra, freeing the rivers Vritra had blocked; the hymn Rigveda 1.32 celebrates the deed. The vajra was made for him by the divine craftsman Tvashtr. Indra's title *Vajrapāṇi*, "thunderbolt in hand", is old. [src: rigveda, wp]

Later Hindu texts (the *Mahabharata* and the Puranas) tell a second origin. When the gods could not defeat the demon Vritra (or the Kalakeyas), they asked the sage Dadhichi for his bones, and he gave up his life willingly; from his spine the vajra was made. The weapon born of a sage's self-sacrifice became a symbol of righteous power. [src: wp]

In Buddhism, the bodhisattva Vajrapani carries the vajra as a protector of the Buddha. In the tantric Buddhism that grew in India from about the seventh century and spread to Tibet, Nepal, Bhutan, China and Japan, the vajra became a ritual implement: a small bronze sceptre with prongs curving together at each end. [src: wp]

## Lifetime

### Ancient

Indra's weapon in the Vedas (c. 1500–1000 BCE), comparable with the thunderbolts of other Indo-European sky gods. In early Buddhist art Vajrapani appears beside the Buddha, sometimes looking like the Greek Heracles in the art of Gandhara. [src: wp]

### Medieval

With the rise of Vajrayana Buddhism, the vajra became one of its two chief ritual objects, paired with the bell (*ghanta*). It travelled to Tibet (where it is called *dorje*) and to Japan, where Shingon Buddhism uses it as the *kongōsho*. In Hindu temples it remained Indra's attribute. [src: wp]

### Early modern

Tibetan monastic ritual, art and architecture made the vajra and the crossed double vajra (*vishvavajra*) everyday sights across the Himalayas. [src: wp]

### Modern

The vajra appears in modern national symbols: four vajras surround the emblem on India's highest military award for gallantry, the Param Vir Chakra; the crossed vajra appears in the emblem of Bhutan. Darjeeling's name is commonly derived from Tibetan *Dorje Ling*, "place of the thunderbolt". [src: wp]

## Today

- **Living ritual:** in Tibetan, Nepalese, Bhutanese and Japanese Buddhism the vajra and bell are used daily in prayer, initiation and meditation.
- **What it means to practitioners:** the vajra stands for skilful means and compassion, the bell for wisdom; held together they show that awakening needs both. The diamond sense means the mind's true nature, which cannot be broken.
- **National emblems:** the Param Vir Chakra medal (India), the emblem of Bhutan.
- **Art and design:** a common motif in Himalayan art, thangka paintings, temple roofs and jewellery.
[src: wp]

## How it was seen

### Vedic: the storm that frees the waters

For the Vedic poets the vajra is the force that breaks the drought: Indra's blow releases the rivers and makes life possible. [src: rigveda]

### Hindu: sacrifice made into strength

The story of Dadhichi teaches that the strongest weapon is made from willing self-giving, and that power used for dharma is holy. [src: wp]

### Buddhist: the diamond mind

In Vajrayana the vajra is "diamond" before it is "thunderbolt": hard, clear, unbreakable, like emptiness and compassion realised together. The thunderbolt sense survives as the sudden, irresistible force of awakening. [src: wp]

### Comparative readings

Mircea Eliade and others treated thunder weapons as signs of the sky's sudden power breaking into the world. Compare the readings of the sky and the centre in the reference books (see "What the books say"). [src: wp]

## Debates

### Thunderbolt or diamond: which came first?

- **Thunderbolt first.** In the Rigveda the vajra is a weapon of storm; "diamond" is a later sense, from the idea of something that cannot be broken. [src: wp]
- **Both at once.** Some scholars hold that the word always joined hardness and brilliance, so the two senses were never far apart. [src: wp]

### One Indo-European storm weapon?

- **A shared inheritance.** Comparative mythologists reconstruct a storm god who strikes a serpent with a weapon (Indra and Vritra, Thor and the Midgard Serpent, Zeus and Typhon). [src: wp]
- **Parallels, not descent.** Others warn that thunder and serpents are common enough that similar myths can arise separately. [src: wp]

## Article

Few words carry as much as *vajra*. It is a weapon, a gem, a philosophy and a bronze object you can hold in one hand. In the Rigveda it is the storm itself, the crack of lightning that splits the cloud-serpent and lets the rivers run. Two thousand years later, in a Tibetan monastery, it is a small sceptre turned in the fingers during prayer, meaning that the nature of mind is as clear and unbreakable as a diamond.

What connects the two is the idea of irresistible force. Indra's blow cannot be parried; awakening, when it comes, cannot be undone. The tantric traditions took the most violent image in the old hymns and turned it inward. The demon to be struck is ignorance; the waters to be freed are compassion.

The vajra never travels alone in Buddhist ritual. It is held with a bell, the vajra in the right hand, the bell in the left. One stands for method, the other for wisdom; one is often called masculine, the other feminine. Neither works without the other. It is a symbol of balance made out of a weapon.

The Hindu story of Dadhichi adds another layer. The gods need a weapon to defeat a demon, and the only material strong enough is the bones of a sage who gives his life for them. Power, in this telling, is borrowed from sacrifice. It is no accident that India's highest award for courage bears four vajras.

For a storyteller the vajra is an object that changes meaning as it changes hands: thunder in a god's fist, a relic of a sage, a monk's tool of meditation. Few objects show so clearly how a culture can keep a symbol and transform what it means.
